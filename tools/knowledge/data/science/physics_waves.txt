# tag: science.physics.waves
what is a wave in physics | what do waves carry | wave definition science => A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from place to place without carrying matter along with it. Sound, light and ocean waves are all examples.
what are transverse waves | transverse wave examples | what is a transverse wave => In a transverse wave, the vibrations go at right angles to the direction the wave travels, like a wave on a rope. Light and water waves are transverse.
what are longitudinal waves | longitudinal wave examples | what is a longitudinal wave => In a longitudinal wave, the vibrations go back and forth in the same direction the wave travels, like a pushed slinky. Sound is a longitudinal wave.
what is wavelength | how do you measure wavelength => Wavelength is the distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave, like crest to crest.
what is frequency | what is hertz | frequency definition physics => Frequency is how many waves pass a point each second. It's measured in hertz (Hz), so 50 Hz means 50 waves per second.
what is amplitude | amplitude of a wave | what does amplitude mean => Amplitude is how big a wave's vibrations are, the height from the middle to a crest. For sound, bigger amplitude means louder.
what is the wave equation | wave speed formula | how do you calculate wave speed => Wave speed = frequency x wavelength (v = fλ). A wave with frequency 10 Hz and wavelength 2 m travels at 20 m/s.
what are crests and troughs | crest and trough of a wave | parts of a wave => The crest is the highest point of a wave and the trough is the lowest point.
what is the period of a wave | time period formula | wave period => The period is the time for one full wave to pass. It's 1 divided by the frequency, so a 2 Hz wave has a period of 0.5 seconds.
what is reflection | what does reflection mean in physics | law of reflection => Reflection is when a wave bounces off a surface. The law of reflection says the angle it hits at equals the angle it bounces off at.
what is refraction | refraction definition => Refraction is a wave bending as it moves from one material to another, because it changes speed. Light slows down in water and glass, so it bends.
why does a straw look bent in water | bent pencil in water => Light from the underwater part bends as it leaves the water, so your eyes see it coming from a different place. That makes straws look broken and pools look shallower.
what is diffraction | what does diffraction mean | why can you hear around corners => Diffraction is waves spreading out as they pass through a gap or around an edge. Sound waves are long, so they diffract a lot, which is why you can hear around corners.
what is interference in waves | what happens when two waves meet | constructive and destructive interference => When waves meet, they add together. Crests lining up make a bigger wave (constructive), and a crest meeting a trough cancels out (destructive).
how do noise cancelling headphones work | noise canceling explained | anc headphones physics => Noise cancelling headphones use microphones to hear outside sound, then play an opposite sound wave. The two waves cancel out, so you hear less noise.
what is absorption of waves | what absorbs sound | what does absorb mean in physics => Absorption is when a material takes in a wave's energy instead of reflecting or passing it on. Soft things like curtains and foam absorb sound well.
what is the electromagnetic spectrum | list the electromagnetic spectrum | em spectrum order => The electromagnetic spectrum from longest to shortest wavelength is radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
how fast do electromagnetic waves travel | do all em waves go the speed of light | speed of radio waves => All electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light in a vacuum, about 300,000 km per second. That includes radio waves, X-rays and visible light.
what are radio waves used for | uses of radio waves | what are radio waves => Radio waves have the longest wavelengths. They carry radio, TV, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and phone signals, and astronomers use them to study space.
what are microwaves used for | uses of microwaves | are microwaves radio waves => Microwaves are short radio waves. They're used in microwave ovens, phone networks, Wi-Fi, satellite communication and weather radar.
what is ultraviolet light | what is uv light | uses of ultraviolet => Ultraviolet (UV) is light just beyond violet that we can't see. It tans and burns skin, makes some things glow, and is used to kill germs.
why is uv light dangerous | does uv cause sunburn | uv and skin cancer => Too much UV can damage skin cells' DNA, causing sunburn and raising the risk of skin cancer. Sunscreen, hats and shade protect you.
what are x rays | how do x rays work | uses of x rays => X-rays are high energy waves that pass through soft tissue but are blocked by bones and metal. That's why bones show up white on an X-ray picture.
who discovered x rays | when were x rays discovered | wilhelm roentgen x rays => Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895. He called them X-rays because they were unknown, and won the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901.
what are gamma rays | uses of gamma rays | are gamma rays dangerous => Gamma rays have the shortest wavelength and the most energy. They come from radioactive atoms and space, and are used to kill cancer cells and sterilize medical tools.
what is visible light | what wavelengths can humans see | visible spectrum range => Visible light is the small part of the electromagnetic spectrum our eyes can see, from red (about 700 nm) to violet (about 400 nm).
what is white light made of | is white light all colors | newton prism white light => White light is a mix of all the colors of the rainbow. Isaac Newton showed this with a prism in the 1660s, splitting sunlight into colors and then joining them back.
what does a prism do | how does a prism split light | why do prisms make rainbows => A prism bends each color of light by a slightly different amount. Violet bends the most and red the least, so white light spreads out into a rainbow of colors.
what is dispersion of light | light dispersion | why do colors separate in glass => Dispersion is white light splitting into its colors because each color bends a different amount when it refracts, like in a prism or raindrops.
why do things have color | how do we see colors | why is a leaf green => Objects reflect some colors of light and absorb others. A leaf absorbs most red and blue light and reflects green, so it looks green.
why is black black | why is white white | do black things reflect light => A white object reflects almost all colors of light. A black object absorbs almost all of them, which is why black things get hot in the sun.
what are the primary colors of light | rgb colors | how do screens make colors => The primary colors of light are red, green and blue. Screens mix tiny red, green and blue dots at different brightness to make every color you see.
paint vs light colors | why is mixing paint different from mixing light => Light colors add together: red, green and blue make white. Paint and ink absorb light, so mixing them subtracts: printers use cyan, magenta and yellow, which mix toward black.
what do red and green light make | mixing colored light | what color is red plus blue light => Mixing light: red and green make yellow, red and blue make magenta, green and blue make cyan, and all three make white.
what is a color filter | how do colored filters work | what does a red filter do => A color filter only lets its own color through and absorbs the rest. A red filter lets red light through and blocks green and blue.
what is a mirror | how do mirrors work | why do mirrors reflect => A mirror is a very smooth, shiny surface, usually a thin layer of metal behind glass. It reflects light in an orderly way, so you see a clear image.
why are mirror images backwards | do mirrors flip left and right => A mirror actually flips front and back, not left and right. Your reflection's right hand looks like a left hand because it's facing you.
what is a concave mirror | uses of concave mirrors | what does a concave mirror do => A concave mirror curves inward like a spoon's bowl. It can focus light and make magnified images, so it's used in makeup mirrors, torches and telescopes.
what is a convex mirror | uses of convex mirrors | why are car side mirrors curved => A convex mirror bulges outward. It shows a wider view but makes things look smaller, which is why it's used for car side mirrors and shop security mirrors.
what is a lens | how do lenses work | what does a lens do => A lens is curved glass or plastic that bends light to form an image. Lenses are used in glasses, cameras, microscopes and telescopes.
what is a convex lens | converging lens uses | what does a magnifying glass do => A convex (converging) lens is thicker in the middle. It bends light inward to a focus and can magnify things, like a magnifying glass.
what is a concave lens | diverging lens uses | what is a diverging lens => A concave (diverging) lens is thinner in the middle. It spreads light out, and it's used in glasses for people who are short sighted.
focal length meaning | what is the focus of a lens => The focal point is where a lens or mirror brings parallel light rays together. The focal length is the distance from the lens to that point.
how do glasses fix your eyesight | how do glasses work | short sighted vs long sighted => Glasses bend light so it focuses properly on your retina. Short sighted people use concave lenses and long sighted people use convex lenses.
how does the eye see | how does light get into your eye | eye physics => Light enters the eye through the pupil, and the lens focuses it onto the retina at the back. The retina turns light into signals that your brain reads as images.
how does a camera work | how does a camera take a picture | camera physics => A camera lens focuses light onto a sensor (or film). The sensor records how much light hits each tiny spot, building up a picture.
what is a pinhole camera | how to make a pinhole camera | why is a pinhole image upside down => A pinhole camera is a dark box with a tiny hole. Light rays cross as they go through the hole, so they make an upside down image on the back.
how does a telescope work | refracting vs reflecting telescope | how do telescopes make things bigger => Telescopes collect lots of light and focus it. Refracting telescopes use lenses, while reflecting telescopes use curved mirrors. An eyepiece then magnifies the image.
how do microscopes magnify | light microscope physics => A light microscope uses two sets of lenses, the objective and the eyepiece, to magnify tiny things. Their magnifications multiply, so 10x times 40x gives 400x.
what is total internal reflection | how do fiber optics work | how does light travel in optical fiber => When light inside glass hits the edge at a steep enough angle, it all reflects back in. Fiber optic cables use this to carry light signals for the internet over long distances.
what is a laser | how do lasers work | what does laser stand for => LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Lasers make a narrow beam of light of one color, used in barcode scanners, surgery and cutting machines.
why are lasers dangerous to eyes | can a laser pointer hurt your eyes | laser eye safety => A laser concentrates light into a tiny spot. Even a small laser pointer can damage your retina if it shines in your eye, so never point lasers at people or planes.
what is a shadow | how are shadows formed | why do shadows happen => A shadow forms when an object blocks light. Light travels in straight lines, so it can't bend around the object to fill in the dark area.
why do shadows change length during the day | why are shadows long in the evening | shadow length and sun => When the Sun is low in the morning and evening, light hits objects at a shallow angle, making long shadows. At midday the Sun is high, so shadows are short.
what is transparent translucent opaque | transparent vs translucent => Transparent things let light through clearly, like glass. Translucent things let some light through but blur it, like frosted glass. Opaque things block light, like wood.
what is luminous | luminous vs non luminous | is the moon luminous => Luminous objects make their own light, like the Sun, fire and light bulbs. Non-luminous objects, like the Moon, are only seen because they reflect light.
why do we see lightning before thunder | why is thunder delayed | light faster than sound => Light travels about a million times faster than sound. You see the flash almost instantly, but the thunder takes about 3 seconds to travel each kilometer.
what is sound | how does sound travel | how is sound made => Sound is a vibration that travels as a wave through air, water or solids. A vibrating object pushes on nearby particles, which push on the next ones.
why cant sound travel in a vacuum | does sound need air | can sound travel without a medium => Sound needs particles to pass the vibration along. A vacuum has no particles, so there's nothing to carry the sound.
does sound travel faster in solids | which material does sound travel fastest in => Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. It goes about 5,900 m/s in steel, 1,500 m/s in water and 343 m/s in air.
what makes a high pitched sound | pitch and frequency => Pitch is how high or low a sound is. It depends on frequency: fast vibrations make high pitched sounds and slow vibrations make low ones.
what is loudness | what makes sounds louder | volume and amplitude => Loudness depends on the amplitude of the sound wave. Bigger vibrations carry more energy and sound louder.
what is a decibel | how is loudness measured | how loud is too loud => Loudness is measured in decibels (dB). Normal talking is about 60 dB, a rock concert can be 110 dB, and long exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing.
what is the range of human hearing | what frequencies can humans hear | highest sound humans can hear => Young people can hear from about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. As people get older, they slowly lose the ability to hear the highest pitches.
what is ultrasound | how is ultrasound used | what is ultrasonic => Ultrasound is sound too high pitched for humans to hear, above 20,000 Hz. It's used to see babies in the womb, clean jewelry and by bats to find prey.
what is infrasound | sounds too low to hear | do elephants use infrasound => Infrasound is sound too low for humans to hear, below 20 Hz. Elephants and whales use it to communicate over long distances, and volcanoes and earthquakes make it too.
how do bats use echolocation | what is echolocation | how do dolphins find food with sound => Echolocation means sending out sounds and listening for echoes. Bats and dolphins use the time and direction of echoes to find food and avoid obstacles.
how does sonar work | what does sonar stand for => Sonar (sound navigation and ranging) sends sound pulses through water and times the echoes. Ships use it to measure depth and find submarines, fish and shipwrecks.
how do you calculate distance from an echo | echo distance formula | sonar depth calculation => Distance = speed x time / 2, because the sound travels there and back. If a sonar echo returns after 2 seconds in water (1,500 m/s), the seabed is 1,500 m deep.
what is the doppler effect | why does a siren change pitch | doppler effect example => The Doppler effect is the change in pitch when a sound source moves. An ambulance sounds higher as it comes toward you and lower as it moves away.
what is a sonic boom | why do jets make a boom | what happens when you break the sound barrier => A sonic boom happens when something goes faster than sound. Its sound waves pile up into a shock wave, which you hear as a loud boom as it passes you.
who first broke the sound barrier | first supersonic flight | chuck yeager bell x 1 => Chuck Yeager first flew faster than sound in level flight on 14 October 1947, in the Bell X-1 rocket plane.
what is mach 1 | what does mach mean | how fast is mach 2 => Mach 1 is the speed of sound, about 1,225 km/h in air at sea level at 15 °C. Mach 2 is twice the speed of sound.
how do musical instruments make sound | how does a guitar make sound | instrument physics => Instruments make something vibrate: strings on guitars and violins, air columns in flutes and trumpets, or skins on drums. The body or tube amplifies the sound.
why do shorter strings make higher notes | guitar string pitch | how to change pitch on a string => Shorter, tighter or thinner strings vibrate faster, making higher notes. That's why pressing a guitar string against a fret raises the pitch.
why does a bottle make a sound when you blow across it | blowing over a bottle pitch | water bottle music => Blowing across the top makes the air inside vibrate. Adding water makes the air column shorter, so the note gets higher.
what is resonance | resonance examples | can a singer break a glass => Resonance is when something is pushed at its natural frequency and starts vibrating strongly. A singer can, rarely, shatter a wine glass by singing its exact note very loudly.
what is an oscilloscope | how do you see sound waves | sound wave on a screen => An oscilloscope shows a signal as a wavy line on a screen. With a microphone, you can see a sound's frequency (how close the waves are) and amplitude (how tall).
why does my voice sound different on a recording | why do i hate my recorded voice | recorded voice sounds weird => When you talk, you hear your voice partly through the bones in your head, which makes it sound deeper. A recording only has the sound through the air.
why does helium make your voice squeaky | helium voice physics | does helium change your pitch => Sound travels faster in helium than in air. That changes how your throat shapes the sound, making your voice sound higher and squeakier. Breathing helium directly can be dangerous.
how do soundproof rooms work | how to soundproof a room | what blocks sound best => Soundproofing uses heavy, dense walls to block sound, gaps of air or insulation, and soft materials to absorb echoes. Sealing gaps around doors helps a lot.
what is an anechoic chamber | quietest room in the world | room with no echo => An anechoic chamber is a room covered in foam wedges that absorb almost all sound, so there are no echoes. Some are so quiet you can hear your own heartbeat.
how do we hear | how does the ear work | how do ears turn sound into signals => Sound waves make your eardrum vibrate. Tiny bones pass the vibrations to the cochlea, where hair cells turn them into nerve signals for your brain.
what does a microphone do | microphone physics => A microphone turns sound into an electrical signal. Sound waves vibrate a thin diaphragm, and that movement makes a changing current.
what is the speed of light exactly | how fast is light in km per second | speed of light in miles per second => Light travels at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second in a vacuum. That's about 300,000 km or 186,000 miles per second.
can anything go faster than light | is light the fastest thing | faster than light travel => According to Einstein's relativity, nothing with mass can reach the speed of light, and no information can travel faster than light.
does light slow down in water | speed of light in glass | is light slower in water => Yes. Light travels about 225,000 km/s in water and about 200,000 km/s in glass, slower than in a vacuum. That slowing causes refraction.
how long does light take to go around the earth | how fast is light around the world | light around earth time => Light could travel around Earth about 7.5 times in one second.
is light a wave or a particle | wave particle duality | what are photons => Light acts like both. It spreads and interferes like a wave, but it also comes in tiny packets of energy called photons.
what is a photon | photon definition | do photons have mass => A photon is a tiny packet of light energy. Photons have no mass and always travel at the speed of light.
why is the sunset red | why does the sky turn red at sunset => At sunset, sunlight passes through much more air to reach you. Most blue light gets scattered away, leaving reds and oranges.
why does the sea look blue | why is water blue => Water absorbs a little more red light than blue, so deep water looks blue. The sea also reflects the blue sky.
why are clouds gray | what color are clouds => Cloud droplets scatter all colors of light equally, so clouds look white. Thick clouds block more light, so their bottoms look gray.
why is the second rainbow reversed | how do double rainbows form => A double rainbow forms when light reflects twice inside raindrops. The outer rainbow is fainter and its colors are in reverse order.
can you ever reach the end of a rainbow | where does a rainbow end | is a rainbow a full circle => No. A rainbow isn't an object in one place; it depends on where you're standing, so it moves as you move. From a plane, you can sometimes see it as a full circle.
what is fluorescence | what makes things glow under black light | fluorescent meaning => Fluorescent materials absorb invisible UV light and give it out as visible light. That's why white shirts, highlighters and some minerals glow under a black light.
how do glow in the dark things work | what is phosphorescence | why do glow stars fade => Glow in the dark materials are phosphorescent. They soak up light energy and release it slowly as a glow, which fades over minutes or hours.
what is polarized light | how do polarized sunglasses work | why do polarized glasses cut glare => Polarized sunglasses only let through light waves vibrating in one direction. Glare reflecting off water and roads is mostly vibrating sideways, so it gets blocked.
how do 3d glasses work | how do 3d movies work | 3d film glasses => 3D glasses send a slightly different picture to each eye, using polarizing filters or colored lenses. Your brain combines them into one image with depth.
what is an optical illusion | why do optical illusions work | how does the brain get tricked => Optical illusions trick your brain, which makes quick guesses about what it sees. Colors, lines and patterns can make it see motion, depth or sizes that aren't really there.
what is persistence of vision | how do movies look like they move | frames per second and eyes => Movies are a series of still pictures shown quickly, usually 24 per second. Your brain blends them together, so they look like smooth motion.
what is seismic waves | what waves do earthquakes make | p waves and s waves => Earthquakes send out seismic waves. P waves are fast, longitudinal waves that travel through solids and liquids. S waves are slower, transverse and can't travel through liquids.
how did scientists learn earth has a liquid core | s waves and the outer core => S waves can't travel through liquid, and they don't reach the far side of Earth after big earthquakes. That shadow showed scientists that Earth's outer core is liquid.
what is a wave in the ocean made of | do ocean waves move water forward | how do ocean waves move => In an ocean wave, the water mostly moves in circles and stays roughly in place. It's the energy that travels across the ocean, usually started by wind.
what is radiation | is all radiation dangerous | types of radiation => Radiation is energy traveling as waves or particles. Some, like visible light and radio waves, is harmless. Ionizing radiation, like X-rays, gamma rays and radioactive particles, can damage cells.
is wifi radiation dangerous | are phone signals harmful | do radio waves cause cancer => Wi-Fi and phone signals use low energy radio waves that can't break chemical bonds or damage DNA. Large studies haven't found that normal use causes health problems.
what is a spectrum | what is spectroscopy | how do scientists know what stars are made of => A spectrum is light spread out into its colors. Each element absorbs and gives off certain colors, like a barcode, so scientists can tell what stars are made of from their light.
what is a hologram | how do holograms work | are holograms real => A hologram is a recording of light that shows a 3D image that changes as you move. It's made using lasers. Many so called holograms in shows are actually other tricks.

what is red eye in photos | why do eyes look red in flash photos | red eye cause => Red eye happens when a camera flash reflects off the blood-rich back of the eye. It's worse in dim light when pupils are wide open.
