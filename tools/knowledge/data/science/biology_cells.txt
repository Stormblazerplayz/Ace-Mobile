# tag: science.bio.cells
who discovered cells | who first saw cells | robert hooke cells => Robert Hooke named cells in 1665 after seeing tiny boxes in a slice of cork under his microscope. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was the first to see living cells and bacteria in the 1670s.
what is cell theory | three parts of cell theory | cell theory statements => Cell theory says all living things are made of one or more cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from other cells.
who came up with cell theory | schleiden and schwann | virchow cells come from cells => Matthias Schleiden (plants, 1838) and Theodor Schwann (animals, 1839) proposed that living things are made of cells. Rudolf Virchow added in 1855 that cells come from other cells.
smallest living thing | what is mycoplasma => Some of the smallest cells are bacteria like Mycoplasma, around 0.2 to 0.3 micrometers wide, too small to see with an ordinary light microscope.
what is the largest cell | biggest single cell | is an egg a cell => An ostrich egg yolk is often called the largest single cell. Some nerve cells are the longest, like those running down a giraffe's neck, over 2 meters long.
what is a prokaryote | what is a eukaryote | prokaryotic vs eukaryotic cells => Prokaryotic cells, like bacteria, are simple and have no nucleus. Eukaryotic cells, like those in plants, animals and fungi, are bigger and keep their DNA inside a nucleus.
what are organelles | list of organelles | organelle meaning => Organelles are tiny structures inside cells that each do a job, like the nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, chloroplasts and vacuoles. The name means little organs.
what is the nucleus | nucleus function => The nucleus is the cell's control center. It holds the DNA, which carries the instructions for building and running the cell.
what is the nucleolus | nucleolus function | what does the nucleolus make => The nucleolus is a dense spot inside the nucleus that makes ribosomes.
what is the nuclear membrane | nuclear envelope | what are nuclear pores => The nuclear envelope is a double membrane around the nucleus. Tiny nuclear pores let messages like RNA move in and out.
do mitochondria have their own dna | why do mitochondria have dna | endosymbiotic theory => Yes. Mitochondria have their own small DNA because they were once free-living bacteria swallowed by an ancestor cell over a billion years ago. This is the endosymbiotic theory.
who proposed endosymbiotic theory | lynn margulis | where did chloroplasts come from => Lynn Margulis championed the endosymbiotic theory in 1967: mitochondria and chloroplasts came from bacteria that started living inside other cells.
what are ribosomes | what do ribosomes do | ribosome function => Ribosomes are tiny machines that build proteins by linking amino acids in the order the cell's genes spell out. Every living cell has them.
what is the endoplasmic reticulum | rough er vs smooth er | what does the er do => The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of folded membranes. Rough ER is dotted with ribosomes and makes proteins. Smooth ER makes fats and breaks down some toxins.
what is the golgi apparatus | what does the golgi body do | golgi function => The Golgi apparatus packages and ships proteins and fats, like a cell's post office. It sorts them and sends them where they're needed, inside or outside the cell.
what are lysosomes | what do lysosomes do | cell recycling center => Lysosomes are small sacs full of digestive enzymes that break down worn-out cell parts, food particles and germs. They're the cell's recycling center.
what is a vacuole | what does a vacuole do | why do plant cells have big vacuoles => A vacuole is a storage sac for water, food or waste. Plant cells have one big central vacuole that pushes against the wall to keep the plant firm.
what is the cell membrane made of | phospholipid bilayer | fluid mosaic model => The membrane is a double layer of phospholipids with proteins floating in it. Their water-loving heads face out and water-fearing tails face in, described by the fluid mosaic model.
what is the cell wall | what is the cell wall made of | which cells have a cell wall => The cell wall is a stiff layer outside the membrane that supports and protects the cell. Plant walls are made of cellulose, fungal walls of chitin. Animal cells have none.
what is cytoplasm | what is the cytosol | cytoplasm function => Cytoplasm is everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus: the jelly-like fluid called cytosol and all the organelles floating in it. Many chemical reactions happen there.
what is the cytoskeleton | what holds a cell's shape | microtubules => The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibers that gives cells shape, holds organelles in place and helps cells move and divide.
what are centrioles | what do centrioles do | do plant cells have centrioles => Centrioles are small tube-shaped structures in animal cells that help organize the fibers that pull chromosomes apart during cell division. Most plant cells don't have them.
what are cilia and flagella | what does a flagellum do | cell tails => Cilia are short hair-like parts that beat to move cells or move fluid past them. A flagellum is a long whip-like tail that swims a cell along, like a sperm cell.
what is the difference between plant and animal cells | plant cell vs animal cell => Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a big central vacuole, and are often boxy. Animal cells have no wall or chloroplasts, have small vacuoles and are often rounder.
what do plant and animal cells have in common | same parts in plant and animal cells | shared organelles => Both have a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
what are the parts of a bacterial cell | what is inside a bacterium | bacteria cell structure => A bacterial cell has a membrane, usually a cell wall, cytoplasm, ribosomes and a loop of DNA floating freely. Many also have a flagellum and tiny rings of DNA called plasmids.
what is a plasmid | plasmids in bacteria | what are plasmids used for => A plasmid is a small ring of DNA in bacteria, separate from the main DNA. Bacteria share them, which spreads traits like antibiotic resistance. Scientists use them to put genes into bacteria.
what is a specialized cell | examples of specialized cells | why do cells have different shapes => Specialized cells have shapes suited to their jobs, like long nerve cells for sending signals, red blood cells with no nucleus to carry more oxygen, and root hair cells for soaking up water.
what are the levels of organization | cell tissue organ system organism | levels of life => Living things are organized from cells, to tissues (groups of similar cells), to organs, to organ systems, to the whole organism.
what is a tissue | types of tissue | tissue in biology => A tissue is a group of similar cells working together. Animals have four main types: muscle, nerve, epithelial (covering) and connective tissue like bone and blood.
what is diffusion | diffusion in cells | diffusion example => Diffusion is when particles spread from where there are lots of them to where there are fewer. Oxygen diffuses into cells this way, with no energy needed.
what is osmosis | osmosis in cells | osmosis definition => Osmosis is when water moves across a membrane from where it's more dilute to where it's more concentrated. It's how plant roots soak up water.
what happens to a cell in salt water | what happens to a cell in pure water | hypertonic hypotonic => In very salty water, a cell loses water and shrinks. In pure water, water rushes in, so animal cells can burst, while plant cells swell and become firm.
what is the gummy bear experiment | osmosis experiment for kids | potato osmosis experiment => Put gummy bears or potato slices in plain water and salt water overnight. In plain water they swell by osmosis, and in salt water potatoes go floppy as they lose water.
what is active transport | active vs passive transport | what is the sodium potassium pump => Active transport uses energy to move substances against the flow, from low to high concentration. Passive transport, like diffusion and osmosis, needs no energy.
what is endocytosis | what is exocytosis | how do cells swallow things => In endocytosis, a cell wraps its membrane around something and pulls it in. In exocytosis, it pushes things out, like hormones, by merging a sac with its membrane.
what is phagocytosis | how do white blood cells eat bacteria | cell eating => Phagocytosis means cell eating. White blood cells called phagocytes wrap around bacteria, swallow them and digest them with lysosomes.
what is atp | what does atp stand for | atp energy => ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the molecule cells use to carry and spend energy. Breaking off one of its phosphate groups releases energy for muscles, nerves and building molecules.
what is cellular respiration | cellular respiration equation | how do cells get energy => Cellular respiration is how cells release energy from food. Glucose plus oxygen becomes carbon dioxide, water and energy stored as ATP. It mostly happens in mitochondria.
what is anaerobic respiration | respiration without oxygen | anaerobic vs aerobic => Anaerobic respiration releases energy without oxygen but makes much less ATP. In muscles it makes lactic acid, and in yeast it makes alcohol and carbon dioxide.
what is fermentation | how does yeast ferment | fermentation examples => Fermentation is when microbes like yeast or bacteria break down sugar without oxygen. It's used to make bread, yogurt, cheese, pickles and soy sauce.
why do muscles burn when you exercise | muscle burn science => During hard exercise, muscles use anaerobic respiration and build up lactate and acid, which helps cause the burning feeling. It clears within minutes to hours after you stop.
how do cells divide | what is cell division | why do cells divide => Cells divide to grow, repair and reproduce. In mitosis, one cell copies its DNA and splits into two identical cells.
what is mitosis | what are the stages of mitosis | pmat => Mitosis splits a nucleus into two identical ones. Its stages are prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase (PMAT), followed by cytokinesis, when the cell itself splits.
what is prophase | what is metaphase | what is anaphase and telophase => In prophase, chromosomes coil up and become visible. In metaphase, they line up in the middle. In anaphase, copies are pulled apart, and in telophase, new nuclei form.
what is interphase | what does a cell do before dividing | cell cycle phases => Interphase is the long stage between divisions, when the cell grows, does its normal job and copies its DNA. Most cells spend most of their time in interphase.
what is the cell cycle | cell cycle explained | how long is the cell cycle => The cell cycle is a cell's life from one division to the next: interphase (growth and DNA copying) then mitosis and cytokinesis. In many human cells it takes about 24 hours.
what is meiosis | why is meiosis important | meiosis vs mitosis => Meiosis makes sex cells (eggs and sperm) with half the normal chromosomes, in four cells that are all different. Mitosis makes two identical cells with the full set.
how many chromosomes do sex cells have | chromosomes in sperm and egg | haploid vs diploid => Human eggs and sperm are haploid, with 23 chromosomes each. When they join, the fertilized egg is diploid, with 46.
what is cancer at the cell level | how does cancer start | why do cancer cells keep dividing => Cancer starts when mutations damage the genes that control cell division, so a cell divides over and over without stopping. The extra cells can form a tumor and spread.
why are stem cells special | types of stem cells => Stem cells are unspecialized cells that can divide and turn into other cell types. Embryonic stem cells can become almost any cell, while adult stem cells, like in bone marrow, make a few types.
how long do cells live | do cells die | lifespan of cells => It depends on the cell. Gut lining cells last a few days, red blood cells about 120 days, and many brain cells can last your whole life.
what is apoptosis | programmed cell death | why do cells self destruct => Apoptosis is programmed cell death, when a cell neatly self-destructs. It removes damaged cells and shapes the body, like separating fingers in an unborn baby.
what is a light microscope | how does a microscope work | microscope magnification => A light microscope uses glass lenses to bend light and magnify things, up to about 1,000 to 1,500 times, enough to see cells and some bacteria.
what is an electron microscope | electron vs light microscope | how strong is an electron microscope => An electron microscope uses beams of electrons instead of light and can magnify over a million times, enough to see viruses and the parts inside cells.
what are the parts of a microscope | microscope parts and functions | eyepiece objective stage => A microscope has an eyepiece lens, objective lenses, a stage to hold the slide, a light source, and focus knobs: coarse for big moves and fine for sharp focus.
how do you work out magnification | total magnification microscope | eyepiece times objective => Multiply the eyepiece lens by the objective lens. A 10x eyepiece with a 40x objective gives 400x magnification.
how do you make a microscope slide | how to look at onion cells | cheek cell slide => Peel a thin layer of onion skin or gently scrape the inside of your cheek, place it on a slide with a drop of water or stain like iodine or methylene blue, and cover it with a coverslip.
why do you stain cells | what does iodine stain | microscope stains => Most cell parts are see-through, so stains add color to make them visible. Iodine stains starch, and methylene blue stains the nucleus in animal cells.
what is a micrometer | how small are cells | units for measuring cells => A micrometer (µm) is a thousandth of a millimeter. Most animal cells are about 10 to 30 µm across and bacteria are about 1 to 2 µm.
what is a unicellular organism | single celled organisms examples | multicellular vs unicellular => Unicellular organisms are made of one cell, like bacteria, amoebas and yeast. Multicellular organisms, like plants and animals, are made of many cells working together.
what is an amoeba | how does an amoeba move | amoeba facts => An amoeba is a single-celled organism that changes shape constantly. It moves and catches food by pushing out arm-like bulges called pseudopods.
what is a paramecium | how does a paramecium move | paramecium facts => A paramecium is a slipper-shaped single-celled organism that lives in ponds. It's covered in tiny hairs called cilia that beat to swim it along.
what is euglena | is euglena a plant or animal | euglena facts => Euglena is a single-celled organism with a flagellum and chloroplasts. It can make food by photosynthesis like a plant but can also eat food like an animal.
what are the molecules of life | four biomolecules | carbohydrates lipids proteins nucleic acids => The four main molecules of life are carbohydrates (energy), lipids or fats (energy and membranes), proteins (structure and enzymes) and nucleic acids like DNA (instructions).
what are proteins made of | what are amino acids | how many amino acids are there => Proteins are long chains of amino acids folded into shapes. Living things use 20 main amino acids, linked in different orders, to make thousands of different proteins.
how do enzymes work | lock and key model => Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions in living things. Each has an active site shaped to fit certain molecules, like a lock and key.
what affects enzymes | why do enzymes stop working when hot | denatured enzyme => Enzymes work best at a certain temperature and pH. Too much heat or the wrong pH changes their shape, called denaturing, so they stop working.
sugars and starches | what is cellulose => Carbohydrates are sugars and chains of sugars, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Glucose is a simple sugar, starch is how plants store energy, and cellulose builds plant cell walls.
what are lipids | what are fats made of | lipids function => Lipids are fats, oils and waxes. They store energy, insulate bodies and make cell membranes. They don't dissolve in water.
how do you test for starch | iodine test for starch | food tests science => Add a few drops of iodine solution. If it turns blue-black, starch is present. If it stays orange-brown, there's no starch.
how do you test for sugar | benedicts test | test for glucose => Heat the food solution with blue Benedict's solution. If a sugar like glucose is present, it turns green, yellow, orange or brick red, depending on how much.
how do you test for protein | biuret test | test for fat => The biuret test turns purple if protein is present. For fat, rub food on paper and let it dry: a see-through greasy spot means fat.
what is homeostasis | homeostasis examples | why is homeostasis important => Homeostasis is how a living thing keeps conditions inside it steady, like body temperature, water and sugar levels, even when the outside changes.
what are the characteristics of living things | how do you know if something is alive | mrs gren => Living things move, respire, sense their surroundings, grow, reproduce, get rid of waste and need nutrition. The letters MRS GREN help you remember them.
is a virus alive | are viruses living things | virus living or nonliving => Viruses are on the border of life. They have genes and evolve, but they can't grow, use energy or reproduce on their own. They need to get inside a living cell to copy themselves.
