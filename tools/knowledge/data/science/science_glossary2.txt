# tag: science.physics.forces
what is newtons first law | first law of motion | law of inertia => Newton's first law says an object stays still, or keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. That's why you lurch forward when a car brakes suddenly.
what is adhesion | what is cohesion | adhesion vs cohesion => Cohesion is molecules of the same substance sticking together, like water forming drops. Adhesion is molecules sticking to a different substance, like water clinging to glass.
what is orbital velocity | how fast does the iss go | how fast do satellites travel => Orbital velocity is the speed needed to keep falling around a planet instead of into it. Near Earth, it's about 7.8 km per second, so the ISS circles Earth in about 90 minutes.
what is microgravity | why do astronauts float => Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are falling around Earth together, not because there's no gravity. At the ISS's height, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground.
# tag: science.physics.waves
why do bones show up on x rays | are x rays dangerous => X-rays are high-energy light that passes through soft tissue but is blocked more by dense bone, which leaves a shadow on the detector. Medical X-rays use small doses, but too much can damage cells.
what is heat radiation | infrared heat from the sun => Heat radiation is heat carried by electromagnetic waves, mostly infrared. It needs no material to travel through, which is how the Sun's heat crosses empty space to reach Earth.
what is a refracting telescope | how does a reflecting telescope work => A refracting telescope uses lenses to bend and focus light, and a reflecting telescope uses curved mirrors. Big research telescopes are reflectors because huge mirrors are easier to make and support than huge lenses.
# tag: science.physics.electricity
what is alternating current | what is direct current | ac vs dc => Direct current (DC) flows one way, like from a battery. Alternating current (AC) keeps reversing direction, 50 or 60 times a second in home outlets, because it's easier to send long distances.
which end of a magnet is north | how do you know the north pole of a magnet => A magnet's north pole is the end that points north when it can swing freely, like a compass needle. Like poles push apart and opposite poles pull together.
# tag: science.chem.atoms
what is a cation | what is an anion | cation vs anion => A cation is a positive ion, an atom that lost electrons, like Na⁺. An anion is a negative ion that gained electrons, like Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract to form ionic compounds such as salt.
what are valence electrons | why do valence electrons matter | outer shell electrons => Valence electrons are the electrons in an atom's outer shell. They decide how an element reacts and bonds, which is why elements in the same group behave alike.
how to speed up a chemical reaction | rate of a reaction => Reactions speed up with higher temperature, higher concentration, more surface area (smaller pieces) and a catalyst. That's why powdered sugar dissolves faster than a sugar cube.
what is an alkane | what is an alkene | alkane vs alkene => Alkanes are hydrocarbons with only single bonds, like methane and propane. Alkenes have at least one carbon-carbon double bond, like ethene, which makes them more reactive and useful for making plastics.
what is crystallization | how to grow crystals from a solution | crystallisation method => Crystallization is when a dissolved solid comes back out as crystals, usually as the solvent cools or evaporates. Slow cooling or evaporation gives bigger crystals.
what is decanting | how to separate sand and water | pouring off a liquid => Decanting is carefully pouring a liquid off a solid that has settled at the bottom. It's a quick way to separate sand from water, though filtering does it better.
what is a centrifuge | how does a centrifuge work | why do labs spin samples => A centrifuge spins samples very fast so heavier parts are pushed to the bottom. Labs use it to separate blood cells from plasma.
what is a greenhouse gas | examples of greenhouse gases | which gases cause global warming => Greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere by absorbing infrared radiation. The main ones are water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide.
# tag: science.bio.life
what is a microorganism | examples of microorganisms | are all microbes bad => A microorganism, or microbe, is a living thing too small to see without a microscope, like bacteria, many fungi and protists. Most are harmless or helpful, like the bacteria in yogurt and your gut.
what is an arachnid | arachnid vs insect => Spiders aren't insects but arachnids, with eight legs and two body parts. Insects have six legs and three body parts. Scorpions, ticks and mites are arachnids too.
cell nucleus function | control center of the cell => The nucleus is the cell's control center. It holds the DNA, which carries the instructions for making proteins and running the cell.
# tag: science.earth.geology
longest mountain range in the world | underwater mountain range => A mid-ocean ridge is an underwater mountain chain where tectonic plates pull apart and new seafloor forms. Together, the ridges form the longest mountain range on Earth, about 65,000 km long.
what is a stalactite | what is a stalagmite | stalactite vs stalagmite => Stalactites hang from cave ceilings, and stalagmites grow up from the floor. Both form from dripping water leaving behind calcite, often growing only about a centimeter every century or so.
what causes ocean currents | how do ocean currents work => Ocean currents are huge, steady flows of seawater. Winds drive surface currents, while differences in temperature and saltiness drive deep currents, moving heat around the planet.
what is a temperate forest | temperate forest biome => Temperate forests grow where there are four seasons, warm summers and cold winters. Many trees, like oaks and maples, are deciduous and drop their leaves in autumn.
# tag: science.space.universe
what is a spiral galaxy | what is an elliptical galaxy | types of galaxies => Galaxies come in three main shapes. Spiral galaxies, like the Milky Way, have arms winding around a center. Elliptical galaxies are smooth, oval balls of older stars, and irregular galaxies have no clear shape.
is the universe expanding | who discovered the universe is expanding | how do we know the universe is expanding => Yes. In 1929, Edwin Hubble showed that distant galaxies are moving away from us, faster the farther they are. Space itself is stretching, and since 1998 we've known the expansion is speeding up.
what is the ecliptic | path of the sun across the sky | why are planets in a line => The ecliptic is the path the Sun seems to follow across the sky over a year. The planets and Moon stay close to it because the solar system is fairly flat.
what is a gibbous moon | waxing gibbous | waning gibbous => A gibbous moon is more than half lit but not full. It's waxing gibbous when growing toward full moon and waning gibbous when shrinking after it.
what is a meteoroid | meteoroid vs meteor vs meteorite => A meteoroid is a space rock smaller than about a meter. When it burns up in our air, the streak of light is a meteor. If a piece reaches the ground, it's a meteorite.
what are the southern lights | aurora australis | where can you see the southern lights => The southern lights, or aurora australis, are the Southern Hemisphere's auroras. They're best seen from Antarctica, and sometimes from Tasmania, New Zealand and the tip of South America.
