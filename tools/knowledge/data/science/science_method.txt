# tag: science.method
scientific method steps in order | list the steps of the scientific method => Ask a question, do background research, make a hypothesis, test it with an experiment, analyze the data, draw a conclusion, and share your results. Scientists often loop back and repeat.
what is a hypothesis | how do you write a hypothesis | hypothesis example => A hypothesis is a testable prediction that answers your question. A good one often uses if and then, like: If plants get more light, then they will grow taller.
what is a scientific law | law vs theory | examples of scientific laws => A scientific law describes what always happens, often as a rule or equation, like Newton's law of gravity. A theory explains why it happens. Theories don't grow up into laws.
what is a variable in science | types of variables => A variable is anything that can change in an experiment. There are independent variables (what you change), dependent variables (what you measure) and controlled variables (what you keep the same).
independent variable example | what do you change in an experiment => The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose. If you test how light affects plant growth, the amount of light is the independent variable.
what is a dependent variable | dependent variable example | what do you measure in an experiment => The dependent variable is what you measure to see the effect. In a plant and light experiment, the plant's height is the dependent variable.
what are constants in an experiment | why keep things the same => Controlled variables are things you keep the same so they don't affect the results, like the amount of water, the soil and the type of plant.
what is a fair test | how do you make an experiment fair | fair test meaning => A fair test changes only one variable at a time and keeps everything else the same, so you know what caused any difference.
why repeat an experiment | why do scientists do multiple trials | how many trials should i do => Repeating trials shows whether results are reliable and not just luck or a mistake. Doing at least three trials and taking an average is a good habit.
how do you calculate an average | mean of results | how to find the mean => Add up all your results and divide by how many there are. For 4, 5 and 6 cm, the mean is 15 divided by 3, which is 5 cm.
what to do with an outlier | anomalous result => An outlier is a result that's far off from the others. Check if it was a mistake. If it was, you can repeat that trial, and explain in your report what you did.
qualitative vs quantitative data | types of data => Data is information you collect. Quantitative data is numbers, like 12 cm. Qualitative data is descriptions, like the leaves turned yellow.
what is an observation | observation vs inference | inference meaning => An observation is something you notice with your senses or tools, like the ice melted in 10 minutes. An inference is a conclusion you draw from it, like the room was warm.
what is a prediction in science | prediction vs hypothesis | how to make a prediction => A prediction says what you expect to happen in a specific test. A hypothesis also tries to explain why. If light helps plants grow is a hypothesis, the plant by the window will be taller is a prediction.
what is a conclusion in science | what goes in a conclusion => A conclusion says whether your results supported your hypothesis, uses your data as evidence, and mentions any problems and what you'd try next.
is it bad if my hypothesis was wrong | what if my experiment fails | hypothesis not supported => No. A hypothesis that isn't supported is still a useful result, because you learned something. Scientists say supported or not supported rather than right or wrong.
what makes a good research question | how to choose a science question | testable question => A good question is testable with an experiment you can actually do, has one thing to change and something to measure, like: Does water temperature change how fast sugar dissolves?
can you test this question | untestable question examples => A testable question can be answered by measuring or observing. Which ice cream is best is an opinion, but which ice cream melts fastest can be tested.
what is a line graph used for | bar graph vs line graph | which graph should i use => Use a line graph when your data changes continuously, like temperature over time. Use a bar graph to compare separate categories, like plant height with different fertilizers.
which axis is the independent variable on | what goes on the x axis | how to label a graph => The independent variable goes on the x axis (across the bottom) and the dependent variable goes on the y axis (up the side). Label both with names and units.
what is a data table | how to make a data table | data table for an experiment => A data table organizes results in rows and columns. Put the independent variable in the first column and your trials and average in the next ones, with units in the headings.
what is a pie chart used for | pie chart in science => A pie chart shows parts of a whole, like what percentage of air is each gas. Use it when the pieces add up to 100%.
what is a scatter graph | what is correlation => A scatter graph plots pairs of measurements as dots. A line of best fit shows the overall trend. If both values rise together, that's a positive correlation.
does correlation mean causation | why correlation is not causation => No. Two things can rise together without one causing the other. Ice cream sales and sunburns both rise in summer, but ice cream doesn't cause sunburn: hot sunny weather causes both.
accurate vs precise | precision meaning => Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. You can be precise but still inaccurate if your scale is off.
what is a measurement error | random vs systematic error | sources of error => Random errors are unpredictable small differences, reduced by repeating trials. Systematic errors push every result the same way, like a ruler with a worn end, and need fixing at the source.
what is the si system | metric system in science => The SI, or International System of Units, is the metric system scientists use worldwide. Its base units include the meter, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela.
why do scientists use the metric system | why not use inches => Scientists use metric units because they're based on tens, so converting is easy, and everyone around the world uses the same ones.
what do metric prefixes mean | kilo centi milli meaning | metric prefixes list => Kilo means a thousand, centi means a hundredth and milli means a thousandth. Micro is a millionth and mega is a million. So a kilometer is 1,000 meters.
how do you read a graduated cylinder | how to measure liquids accurately => Put the cylinder on a flat surface and read at eye level. Water curves into a dip called a meniscus, so read the bottom of the curve.
what is volume and how is it measured | how to measure volume of a rock | water displacement method => Volume is how much space something takes up. For an odd-shaped object, put it in a measuring cylinder of water and see how much the water rises. That rise is its volume.
what is mass vs weight in science class | do you measure mass with a scale | balance vs scale => Mass is how much matter something has, measured in grams or kilograms with a balance. Weight is the pull of gravity on it, measured in newtons with a spring scale.
how do you use a triple beam balance | triple beam balance steps | reading a balance => Zero the balance first, put the object on the pan, then slide the riders from biggest to smallest until the pointer lines up with zero. Add up all three readings.
sig figs meaning | why use significant figures => Significant figures are the digits in a measurement that are actually known. They stop you from claiming more precision than your tools gave, like writing 2.4 cm instead of 2.4318 cm from a ruler.
what is peer review | why do scientists check each others work | peer reviewed meaning => Peer review is when other expert scientists check a study before a journal publishes it, looking for mistakes or weak evidence. It helps keep science trustworthy.
what is replication in science | why is reproducibility important | replicate an experiment => Replication is when other scientists repeat an experiment and get the same result. A finding that can't be reproduced isn't trusted.
what is a model in science | types of scientific models | why do scientists use models => A model is a simplified version of something that helps explain or predict it, like a globe, a diagram of an atom, or a computer simulation of weather.
what is a double blind study | what is a placebo | blind experiment => In a double-blind study, neither the people tested nor the researchers know who gets the real treatment and who gets a placebo, a fake one. This stops expectations from affecting results.
what is sample size | why does sample size matter | bigger sample better => Sample size is how many things or people you test. Bigger samples give more reliable results, because odd results average out.
what is bias in science | how to avoid bias in an experiment => Bias is anything that unfairly pushes results one way, like only testing your favorite option or wanting a certain answer. Random choices, controls and blind tests help reduce it.
how do you tell if something is real science | what is pseudoscience | science vs pseudoscience => Real science makes testable claims, changes its mind with new evidence and is checked by others. Pseudoscience sounds scientific but avoids testing or ignores evidence against it, like astrology.
how do you check if a source is reliable | is this website trustworthy | reliable science sources => Check who wrote it and whether they're experts, whether it cites evidence, when it was updated, and whether other reliable sources agree. Science organizations, universities and museums are good places to start.
what is empirical evidence | what counts as evidence in science | evidence based meaning => Empirical evidence comes from observing and measuring the real world, through experiments or observations, not just opinions or beliefs.
how to write a method for an experiment | writing a procedure | step by step method => Write numbered steps clearly enough that someone else could repeat your experiment exactly, including amounts, times and equipment.
what is an evaluation in science | how to improve an experiment | what went wrong in my experiment => An evaluation looks at how good your experiment was: what errors happened, how reliable the results are, and what you'd change to improve it next time.
what is a variable you cant control | what are confounding variables | hidden variable => A confounding variable is something you didn't control that affects your results, like room temperature changing during your test. Try to spot and limit them.
what does a scientist do all day | what do scientists actually do | types of scientists => Scientists plan and run experiments, collect data, analyze it on computers, read other studies, write papers and give talks. Many work in teams in labs, outdoors or with computers.
how do you become a scientist | what to study to be a scientist | can kids be scientists => Be curious, ask questions and test them. Study math and science in school, then a science degree at university. Many kids also join citizen science projects and contribute real data.
what is citizen science | citizen science projects for kids | how can kids help real scientists => Citizen science is when ordinary people help real research, like counting birds, classifying galaxies on Zooniverse or reporting nature sightings in apps like iNaturalist.
what is stem | what does stem stand for | stem vs steam => STEM stands for science, technology, engineering and math. STEAM adds art.
engineering design steps | design process for kids => Engineers define the problem, research, brainstorm ideas, pick one, build a prototype, test it, and improve it. Then they test again.
engineer vs scientist | difference between science and engineering | what does an engineer do => Scientists study how the world works, and engineers use that knowledge to design and build solutions, like bridges, phones and medicines.
what are the branches of science | main types of science | physical life earth science => The main branches are physical science (physics and chemistry), life science (biology) and earth and space science (geology, weather and astronomy).
what is physics | what do physicists study | physics definition => Physics is the science of matter, energy, forces and motion, from tiny particles to the whole universe.
what is chemistry | what do chemists study | chemistry definition => Chemistry is the science of substances: what they're made of, how they behave and how they react to make new substances.
what is biology | what do biologists study | biology definition => Biology is the science of living things: how they're built, how they work, how they grow, how they're related and how they interact.
what is earth science | what do earth scientists study | earth science definition => Earth science studies our planet: rocks, minerals, soil, oceans, weather, climate and the history of Earth.
what is astronomy | what do astronomers study => Astronomy is the science of space and everything in it, like stars, planets and galaxies. Astrology claims the stars affect people's lives, but it isn't science and fails when tested.
what is ecology | what does an ecologist study | ecology definition => Ecology is the study of how living things interact with each other and with their environment.
what is botany | what is zoology | what is microbiology => Botany is the study of plants, zoology is the study of animals, and microbiology is the study of microorganisms like bacteria.
what is paleontology | paleontology vs archaeology => Paleontology studies ancient life through fossils, like dinosaurs. Archaeology studies human history through things people left behind, like tools and buildings.
what is forensic science | how does forensic science work | crime scene science => Forensic science uses science to solve crimes, by studying fingerprints, DNA, fibers, blood and chemicals found at crime scenes.
what is the oldest science | first scientists in history | when did science begin => Astronomy is often called the oldest science, because ancient peoples tracked the Sun, Moon and stars for calendars thousands of years ago. Ancient Greek thinkers began explaining nature with reasons rather than myths.
who is called the father of the scientific method | francis bacon scientific method => Ibn al-Haytham, around 1000 AD, used careful experiments to study light and is often called a pioneer of the scientific method. Francis Bacon and Galileo also helped shape it in the 1600s.
what is occams razor | simplest explanation | occams razor meaning => Occam's razor is the idea that when two explanations fit the facts equally well, the simpler one is usually the better bet.
what is a scientific journal | what is nature journal | where are science discoveries published => A scientific journal publishes peer-reviewed research. Famous ones include Nature, Science and The Lancet. Thousands of journals cover different fields.
what is the nobel prize in science | nobel prize categories | who started the nobel prize => The Nobel Prizes were set up in Alfred Nobel's will and first given in 1901. The science prizes are for Physics, Chemistry, and Physiology or Medicine.
what is a lab notebook | why keep a science journal | science notebook tips => A lab notebook records everything you do in an experiment, with dates, measurements and observations, even mistakes. Scientists never tear out pages, so the record is honest.
what is a hypothesis example for plants | hypothesis examples for kids | sample hypotheses => Examples: If plants are watered with salt water, then they will grow less. If a ball is dropped from higher, then it will bounce higher. If ice is in salt water, then it will melt faster.
