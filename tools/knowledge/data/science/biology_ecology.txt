# tag: science.bio.ecology
what do plants need for photosynthesis | ingredients of photosynthesis | what are the reactants of photosynthesis => Plants need carbon dioxide from the air, water from the soil, light energy from the Sun and chlorophyll to capture it.
which part of the plant does photosynthesis | where in the leaf => Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, mostly in the leaf's palisade cells near the top surface where light is strongest.
what do stomata do | guard cells => Stomata are tiny pores, mostly on the underside of leaves, that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapor out. Guard cells open and close them.
why is chlorophyll green | what does chlorophyll absorb => Chlorophyll is the green pigment that captures light for photosynthesis. It absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green, which is why leaves look green.
what are the light dependent reactions | what is the calvin cycle | two stages of photosynthesis => In the light-dependent reactions, chlorophyll captures light, splits water and releases oxygen. In the Calvin cycle, that energy is used to turn carbon dioxide into sugar.
where does a tree get its mass | where do plants get their mass | do trees grow from soil => Surprisingly, most of a tree's mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air, turned into sugar and wood by photosynthesis. Very little comes from the soil.
what limits photosynthesis | factors affecting photosynthesis | what speeds up photosynthesis => The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide level and temperature. Whichever is in shortest supply limits it.
how do you show plants make oxygen | pondweed experiment | elodea bubbles => Put pondweed like Elodea in water under a lamp and count the oxygen bubbles. Move the lamp closer and the bubbles speed up because there's more light.
how are photosynthesis and respiration related | photosynthesis vs respiration | opposite of photosynthesis => They're opposites. Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and water to make sugar and oxygen. Respiration uses sugar and oxygen to release energy, carbon dioxide and water.
do plants respire | do plants use oxygen => Yes. Plants respire all the time, using oxygen to release energy from sugar. In daylight, they make much more oxygen by photosynthesis than they use.
what do plants do with the glucose they make | how do plants use sugar | plant food storage => Plants use glucose for energy, turn it into cellulose for cell walls and starch for storage, and make proteins, fats and oils from it.
transpiration pull | how do tall trees drink => Water evaporates from leaves, pulling a continuous column of water up through the xylem from the roots, like sucking through a straw.
what is the celery experiment | food coloring in celery | colored flowers experiment => Stand celery or a white flower in water with food coloring. Over a day, the color travels up the xylem tubes, showing how water moves through plants.
how do seeds form | what happens after pollination | fertilization in plants => After pollination, a pollen tube grows down to the ovule and fertilizes it. The ovule becomes a seed, and the ovary around it becomes the fruit.
what is a tomato fruit or vegetable | botanical fruit => A tomato is botanically a fruit, because it grows from a flower and holds seeds. So are cucumbers, peppers and pumpkins. Vegetable is a cooking word, not a science one.
what is an ecosystem | ecosystem definition | parts of an ecosystem => An ecosystem is all the living things in an area plus their nonliving surroundings, like water, soil, air and sunlight, all interacting.
what are biotic and abiotic factors | biotic vs abiotic | examples of abiotic factors => Biotic factors are living things, like plants, animals and bacteria. Abiotic factors are nonliving, like sunlight, temperature, water, soil and air.
what is a population in biology | what is a community in biology | population community ecosystem => A population is all the members of one species in an area. A community is all the populations of different species living there together.
what is a habitat | what is a niche | habitat vs niche => A habitat is where an organism lives. Its niche is its role there: what it eats, what eats it and how it uses its surroundings.
what is a producer | what is a consumer | producer vs consumer => Producers, like plants and algae, make their own food from sunlight. Consumers can't, so they eat other living things.
what are herbivores carnivores and omnivores | herbivore meaning | omnivore meaning => Herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, and omnivores eat both. Cows are herbivores, lions are carnivores, and bears and humans are omnivores.
what are decomposers | what do decomposers do | examples of decomposers => Decomposers, like bacteria and fungi, break down dead plants and animals and waste, returning nutrients to the soil for plants to use again.
what is a detritivore | decomposer vs detritivore | are worms decomposers => Detritivores, like earthworms, woodlice and millipedes, eat bits of dead material. Decomposers, like fungi and bacteria, digest it chemically from outside.
what is a food chain example | food chain order | how does a food chain work => A food chain shows who eats whom, like grass, to grasshopper, to frog, to snake, to hawk. Arrows point in the direction the energy goes.
what is a food web | food web vs food chain | why are food webs important => A food web is many connected food chains in an ecosystem. It shows that most animals eat, and are eaten by, more than one thing.
what are trophic levels | primary secondary tertiary consumers | trophic level meaning => Trophic levels are feeding levels: producers first, then primary consumers (herbivores), secondary consumers, and tertiary consumers. An apex predator is at the top.
what is an apex predator | top of the food chain | examples of apex predators => An apex predator has no natural predators as an adult, like orcas, lions, eagles and great white sharks.
what is the 10 percent rule | why are food chains short | energy pyramid => Only about 10% of the energy at one level passes to the next. The rest is used for living or lost as heat. That's why food chains rarely have more than four or five links.
pyramid of numbers | pyramid of biomass => An energy pyramid shows energy shrinking at each level up the food chain. There's lots of energy in plants, less in herbivores and very little at the top.
what happens if a species is removed from a food web | effects of removing a predator | trophic cascade => Removing one species can ripple through the food web. Removing a predator can let its prey multiply and overeat their food, which is called a trophic cascade.
what happened when wolves returned to yellowstone | yellowstone wolves | wolves change rivers => Wolves were brought back to Yellowstone in 1995. They reduced elk numbers and changed elk behavior, which helped willows and aspens recover in some areas. Scientists still debate how big the effect was.
what is a keystone species | examples of keystone species | sea otters and kelp => A keystone species has a huge effect on its ecosystem compared to its numbers. Sea otters eat sea urchins, and without otters, urchins can wipe out kelp forests.
what is symbiosis | types of symbiosis | mutualism commensalism parasitism => Symbiosis is two species living closely together. In mutualism both benefit, in commensalism one benefits and the other isn't affected, and in parasitism one benefits and the other is harmed.
what is an example of mutualism | clownfish and sea anemone | bees and flowers mutualism => Clownfish and sea anemones are a classic example: the anemone protects the clownfish, and the clownfish cleans and defends it. Bees and flowers help each other too.
what is an example of commensalism | barnacles on whales | commensalism examples => Barnacles riding on whales get a free ride to food, while the whale is mostly unaffected. Birds nesting in trees are another example.
what is an example of parasitism | examples of parasites | what is a parasite => A parasite lives on or in a host and harms it, like ticks, fleas, tapeworms and mistletoe on trees.
what is competition in ecology | competition between species | what do animals compete for => Competition is when living things struggle for the same limited resources, like food, water, light, space or mates.
what is predation | predator prey relationship | predator prey cycle => Predation is when one animal hunts and eats another. Predator and prey numbers often rise and fall in cycles, like lynx and snowshoe hares in Canada.
what is carrying capacity | carrying capacity meaning | what limits populations => Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support with its food, water and space. Limiting factors include food, predators and disease.
what is biodiversity | why is biodiversity important | what threatens biodiversity => Biodiversity is the variety of life in an area. It makes ecosystems stronger and gives us food, medicine and clean air and water. Habitat loss, pollution and climate change threaten it.
what is a biodiversity hotspot | most biodiverse places | which ecosystem has the most species => Tropical rainforests and coral reefs have the most species. Biodiversity hotspots, like Madagascar and the Andes, have many species found nowhere else that are under threat.
what is an invasive species | examples of invasive species | why are invasive species bad => An invasive species is one brought to a new place where it spreads and causes harm. Cane toads in Australia and zebra mussels in North America are examples.
what is a biome | list of biomes | major biomes of the world => A biome is a large region with similar climate, plants and animals. Major land biomes include tropical rainforest, desert, grassland, temperate forest, taiga and tundra.
what is a tropical rainforest biome | why do rainforests have so many species => Tropical rainforests are hot and wet all year, with over 2,000 mm of rain. They have layers (emergent, canopy, understory and forest floor) and hold over half the world's land species.
what is the tundra | tundra biome facts | why are there no trees in the tundra => The tundra is a cold, treeless biome near the Arctic with permafrost under the soil. The short summer and frozen ground let only mosses, lichens, grasses and small shrubs grow.
what is a temperate deciduous forest | deciduous forest biome => Temperate deciduous forests have four seasons and trees like oak and maple that drop their leaves in autumn to save water in winter.
what is a grassland biome | savanna vs prairie | what animals live in grasslands => Grasslands are dominated by grasses with few trees. Tropical savannas, like the Serengeti, have zebras and lions. Temperate prairies and steppes once had huge herds of bison.
how do plants survive in the desert | desert adaptations => Deserts get under about 250 mm of rain a year. Plants like cacti store water in thick stems and have spines instead of leaves to lose less water.
what is a wetland | why are wetlands important | types of wetlands => Wetlands, like marshes, swamps and bogs, are land soaked with water. They filter water, prevent floods, store carbon and shelter many birds and fish.
what is a freshwater ecosystem | ponds lakes and rivers ecosystems | freshwater biome => Freshwater ecosystems include ponds, lakes, rivers and streams. They hold less than 1% of Earth's water but are home to about 10% of known species.
what is ecological succession | primary vs secondary succession | how does a forest grow back => Succession is how a community changes over time. Primary succession starts on bare rock, with lichens first. Secondary succession happens after a fire or clearing, where soil remains.
what is a pioneer species | lichens pioneer species | first organisms to grow => Pioneer species are the first to grow on bare ground, like lichens and mosses. They break down rock and add organic matter, making soil for other plants.
what is a lichen | lichen symbiosis => A lichen isn't a plant. It's a partnership between a fungus and algae or cyanobacteria. The algae make food, and the fungus gives shelter and water.
what is the carbon cycle | how does carbon move | carbon cycle steps => In the carbon cycle, plants take in CO2 for photosynthesis, animals eat plants, and respiration, decay and burning return CO2 to the air. Oceans, rocks and fossil fuels store carbon too.
why do plants need nitrogen | nitrogen fixing bacteria => Plants need nitrogen to make proteins but can't use nitrogen gas from the air. Bacteria in soil and in root nodules of plants like peas and beans fix it into forms plants can use.
what is the oxygen cycle | oxygen cycle explained => Plants and algae release oxygen through photosynthesis. Animals, plants and decomposers use it for respiration and give back carbon dioxide.
what is composting | how does compost work | what can you compost => Composting lets bacteria, fungi and worms break down food scraps and garden waste into rich soil. Fruit and vegetable scraps, leaves and coffee grounds work, but not meat or dairy at home.
what is bioaccumulation | biomagnification | ddt and eagles => Some poisons build up in animals' bodies and get more concentrated up the food chain, called biomagnification. The pesticide DDT thinned eagle eggshells until it was banned in the US in 1972.
who was rachel carson | silent spring | what did rachel carson do => Rachel Carson was a biologist whose 1962 book Silent Spring warned about pesticides like DDT harming wildlife. It helped start the modern environmental movement.
why is deforestation bad | causes of deforestation => Deforestation is clearing forests, mostly for farming, cattle and logging. It destroys habitats, releases carbon dioxide and can cause soil erosion and floods.
what is habitat loss | biggest threat to wildlife | habitat destruction => Habitat loss, from farming, cities and roads, is the biggest threat to wildlife worldwide. Animals lose places to live, feed and breed.
what is an endangered species | endangered vs threatened | iucn red list => An endangered species is at high risk of extinction. The IUCN Red List ranks species from least concern through vulnerable, endangered and critically endangered to extinct.
what is conservation | how can we save endangered animals | conservation examples => Conservation protects nature through parks and reserves, laws against hunting, breeding programs and restoring habitats. The bald eagle and humpback whale have both recovered this way.
what are the 3 rs | reduce reuse recycle | why reduce reuse recycle => The 3 Rs are reduce (use less), reuse (use things again) and recycle (turn waste into new products). Reducing helps most, because it avoids waste in the first place.
does plastic ever decompose | plastic bottle decomposition => A plastic bottle can take hundreds of years to break down, and it never fully disappears. It just breaks into smaller and smaller microplastics.
what are microorganisms | types of microbes | microbes definition => Microorganisms, or microbes, are living things too small to see without a microscope. They include bacteria, archaea, many fungi like yeast, protists and microscopic algae. Viruses are often studied with them.
are all bacteria bad | good bacteria examples | helpful bacteria => Most bacteria are harmless or helpful. They help digest food, make vitamins in your gut, make yogurt and cheese, break down dead things and fix nitrogen for plants.
how fast do bacteria multiply | bacteria doubling time | how bacteria reproduce => Bacteria reproduce by splitting in two, called binary fission. In ideal conditions, some like E. coli can double every 20 minutes.
what shapes are bacteria | cocci bacilli spirilla | bacteria shapes names => Bacteria come in three main shapes: round (cocci), rod-shaped (bacilli) and spiral (spirilla).
how do viruses reproduce | how does a virus infect a cell | virus life cycle => A virus attaches to a cell, injects its genes, and hijacks the cell to make copies of itself. The new viruses burst out or bud off to infect more cells.
how small is a virus | virus vs bacteria size | can you see a virus with a microscope => Most viruses are about 20 to 300 nanometers wide, roughly 10 to 100 times smaller than bacteria. You need an electron microscope to see most of them.
what is a bacteriophage | viruses that infect bacteria | phage => A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. Many look like tiny lunar landers. Doctors are testing them to fight bacteria that resist antibiotics.
what is germ theory | louis pasteur germs | who proved germs cause disease => Germ theory says many diseases are caused by microorganisms. Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch proved it in the 1860s to 1880s, replacing the idea that bad air caused disease.
what is pasteurization | why is milk pasteurized | who invented pasteurization => Pasteurization heats food or drink, like milk, to kill harmful microbes without boiling it. Louis Pasteur developed it in the 1860s, first for wine.
what was the swan neck flask experiment | did pasteur disprove spontaneous generation | spontaneous generation => In 1859, Pasteur boiled broth in flasks with long, curved necks. Dust and microbes got trapped in the bend, and the broth stayed clear, showing life doesn't appear from nothing.
how does mold grow | why does bread go moldy => Mold is a fungus that grows from tiny spores floating in the air. It grows on food that's warm and moist, spreading thread-like hyphae through it.
what is the yeast balloon experiment | yeast and sugar balloon | inflate a balloon with yeast => Mix yeast, sugar and warm water in a bottle and stretch a balloon over the top. As the yeast eats the sugar, its carbon dioxide slowly blows up the balloon.
what is a tardigrade | water bear | toughest animal on earth => Tardigrades, or water bears, are tiny animals about 0.5 mm long. They can survive drying out, freezing, huge pressure, radiation and even the vacuum of space by going into a dried-up state.
what is an extremophile | life in extreme places | bacteria in hot springs => Extremophiles are living things that thrive in extreme conditions, like boiling hot springs, acid, salt lakes or deep rock. Some microbes grow at over 120 °C.
