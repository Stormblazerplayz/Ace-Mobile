what are the layers of the atmosphere | atmosphere layers in order | name the layers of the atmosphere => From the ground up, the layers are the troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere.
what is the troposphere | how high is the troposphere | where does weather happen => The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere, about 8 to 18 km high (highest at the equator). Almost all weather happens here.
what is the stratosphere | why do planes fly in the stratosphere | stratosphere facts => The stratosphere is from about 12 to 50 km up and holds the ozone layer. It's calm and dry, so big jets often fly at its bottom edge.
what is the mesosphere | where do meteors burn up | mesosphere facts => The mesosphere is from about 50 to 85 km up. It's the coldest layer, around minus 90 °C at its top, and most meteors burn up there.
what is the thermosphere | is the thermosphere hot | where does the iss orbit => The thermosphere is from about 85 km to 600 km up. The thin gas there can reach over 1,000 °C, but it wouldn't feel hot because there are so few molecules. The ISS orbits in it.
what is the exosphere | where does the atmosphere end | top layer of the atmosphere => The exosphere is the very thin outermost layer, fading into space thousands of kilometers up.
what is the karman line | where does space begin | how high is space => Space is often said to begin at the Kármán line, 100 km above sea level. The US Air Force and NASA use 80 km (50 miles) for astronaut wings.
what is air made of | gases in the atmosphere | composition of air => Dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, almost 1% argon and about 0.04% carbon dioxide, plus tiny amounts of other gases. It also holds varying amounts of water vapor.
what is air pressure | why does air pressure change | what is atmospheric pressure => Air pressure is the weight of the air above pushing down. At sea level it's about 1,013 hectopascals (millibars). It drops as you go higher and changes with the weather.
what does a barometer measure | how does a barometer work | falling barometer meaning => A barometer measures air pressure. Falling pressure often means clouds and rain are coming, while rising pressure usually means clearer, calmer weather.
what is high pressure weather | what is a high pressure system | anticyclone => A high pressure system, or anticyclone, has sinking air that stops clouds forming, so it usually brings clear, calm, dry weather.
what is low pressure weather | what is a low pressure system | cyclone weather => A low pressure system has rising air that cools and forms clouds, so it usually brings cloudy, windy, rainy weather.
what is a weather front | what is a cold front | what is a warm front => A front is the boundary between two air masses. A cold front pushes under warm air and brings quick, heavy showers. A warm front slides over cold air and brings longer, steadier rain.
what is an air mass | types of air masses | where do air masses come from => An air mass is a huge body of air with similar temperature and humidity throughout. It takes on the character of where it formed, like cold and dry over the Arctic or warm and wet over tropical oceans.
what is humidity | what is relative humidity | why does humid air feel sticky => Humidity is the amount of water vapor in the air. Relative humidity compares it to the most the air could hold at that temperature. High humidity feels sticky because sweat can't evaporate well.
what is dew point | dew point meaning | how does dew form => The dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and water condenses. On clear nights, grass cools below the dew point and dew forms on it.
what is frost | how does frost form | frost vs dew => Frost forms when surfaces cool below freezing and water vapor turns straight into ice crystals. Dew forms the same way but above freezing, as liquid drops.
what is fog | how does fog form | fog vs mist => Fog is a cloud touching the ground, made when moist air cools to its dew point. It's called mist if you can see more than 1 km, and fog if you can see less.
how do clouds form | what are clouds made of | why do clouds form => Clouds form when rising air cools and its water vapor condenses on tiny dust or salt particles. They're made of billions of tiny water droplets or ice crystals.
how much does a cloud weigh | are clouds heavy | weight of a cloud => A typical puffy cumulus cloud holds roughly 500 tonnes of water, but it floats because the droplets are tiny and spread across a huge volume of rising air.
why are clouds white | why are rain clouds dark | why do clouds turn gray => Clouds look white because their droplets scatter all colors of sunlight equally. Thick clouds with lots of big drops block more light, so they look gray or dark from below.
what are cumulus clouds | puffy clouds name | cotton ball clouds => Cumulus clouds are puffy, white clouds with flat bottoms, often seen on sunny days. If they grow tall, they can become cumulonimbus storm clouds.
what are cirrus clouds | wispy clouds | high thin clouds => Cirrus clouds are thin, wispy clouds high up, made of ice crystals. They can be a sign that the weather will change in a day or so.
what are stratus clouds | gray blanket clouds | low flat clouds => Stratus clouds are flat, gray, low layers that can cover the whole sky like a blanket, often bringing drizzle.
what are cumulonimbus clouds | thunderstorm clouds | anvil cloud => Cumulonimbus clouds are huge, tall storm clouds that can reach over 12 km high, often with a flat anvil-shaped top. They bring thunder, lightning, heavy rain and sometimes hail or tornadoes.
what are nimbostratus clouds | rain clouds name | which clouds bring rain => Nimbostratus clouds are thick, dark gray layers that bring long periods of steady rain or snow. Cumulonimbus clouds bring short, heavy showers and storms.
what are altocumulus clouds | mackerel sky | middle level clouds => Altocumulus clouds are middle level clouds in patches or rows of puffy white or gray clumps. A sky full of them looks like fish scales, called a mackerel sky.
what are contrails | why do planes leave white trails | airplane clouds => Contrails are clouds made when hot, wet engine exhaust freezes into ice crystals in the cold air high up. They last longer when the air is humid.
what are mammatus clouds | lenticular clouds | weird cloud shapes => Mammatus clouds hang down like pouches under storm clouds. Lenticular clouds are smooth, lens or UFO shaped clouds that form over mountains.
why does it rain | how do raindrops form => Tiny cloud droplets bump together and grow, or ice crystals grow and melt as they fall. When drops get heavy enough, they fall as rain.
what shape are raindrops | are raindrops tear shaped | raindrop shape => Raindrops aren't tear shaped. Small ones are round, and bigger ones flatten on the bottom like a hamburger bun as they fall.
how fast does rain fall | speed of a raindrop | how fast do raindrops fall => Raindrops fall at about 2 to 9 meters per second, depending on size. Big drops fall fastest.
what is sleet | what is freezing rain | sleet vs freezing rain => Sleet is rain that freezes into ice pellets before it lands. Freezing rain falls as liquid and freezes when it hits cold surfaces, coating everything in ice.
how does snow form | why does it snow => Snow forms when water vapor turns straight into ice crystals in cold clouds. The crystals stick together into snowflakes, which fall if the air is cold all the way down.
can it be too cold to snow | why doesnt it snow when its very cold | coldest snow => It's never truly too cold to snow, but very cold air holds little moisture, so heavy snow is more common when it's near freezing.
what is a blizzard | blizzard definition | blizzard vs snowstorm => A blizzard is a snowstorm with strong winds of at least 56 km/h and blowing snow that cuts visibility to under 400 meters, lasting three hours or more.
what is lake effect snow | why do great lakes get so much snow | lake effect => Lake effect snow happens when cold air crosses a warmer lake, picks up moisture and dumps heavy snow on the shore downwind. Areas near the Great Lakes get a lot of it.
why does hail happen in summer | biggest hailstone => Hail forms in strong thunderstorms when updrafts carry raindrops up into freezing air again and again, adding ice layers. The biggest US hailstone, from South Dakota in 2010, was 20 cm across.
how does lightning form | why is there lightning => In storm clouds, ice and water bump together and build up electric charges. When the difference gets big enough, a giant spark jumps between the cloud and the ground or within clouds.
how hot is lightning | lightning temperature | is lightning hotter than the sun => Lightning can heat the air to about 30,000 °C, around five times hotter than the surface of the Sun.
how often does lightning strike | how many lightning strikes per second | lightning per day => Lightning strikes Earth about 40 to 50 times every second, around 1.4 billion flashes a year.
does lightning strike the same place twice | can lightning strike twice | empire state building lightning => Yes. Tall objects get hit often. The Empire State Building is struck around 20 to 25 times a year.
count seconds after lightning | lightning distance rule => Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder. Divide by 3 for kilometers or by 5 for miles, because sound travels about 1 km every 3 seconds.
what are the odds of getting struck by lightning | chance of being hit by lightning | lightning odds => In the US, the chance of being struck in a given year is about 1 in 1.2 million. Most people who are struck survive.
what is ball lightning | is ball lightning real | rare lightning types => Ball lightning is a rare, glowing ball reported during storms that floats for a few seconds. It's real but poorly understood, and scientists still debate what causes it.
what is a supercell | supercell thunderstorm | rotating thunderstorm => A supercell is a powerful thunderstorm with a deep, rotating updraft called a mesocyclone. Supercells cause most strong tornadoes and big hail.
how do tornadoes form | what causes a tornado to form | tornado formation => Tornadoes usually form from supercell thunderstorms when wind changing speed and direction with height creates spinning air that gets tilted upright by the storm's updraft.
what is the enhanced fujita scale | how are tornadoes rated | ef5 tornado => Tornadoes are rated EF0 to EF5 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, based on damage. EF5 tornadoes have winds over 322 km/h (200 mph).
what is tornado alley | where do most tornadoes happen | which country has the most tornadoes => The United States has more tornadoes than anywhere else, about 1,200 a year. Tornado Alley in the central plains, from Texas to Nebraska, gets many of them.
what should you do in a tornado | where to hide in a tornado => Go to a basement or storm shelter, or an inside room with no windows on the lowest floor. Cover your head. Mobile homes and cars aren't safe.
what is a tornado watch vs warning | watch vs warning weather | difference between watch and warning => A watch means conditions are right for the danger to form, so be ready. A warning means it's happening or about to, so take cover now.
what is a waterspout | tornado over water | waterspout facts => A waterspout is a spinning column of air over water, like a tornado. Most are weaker than land tornadoes.
what is a dust devil | dust devil vs tornado | small whirlwinds => A dust devil is a small whirlwind made by hot ground heating the air on sunny days. Unlike tornadoes, they don't come from storm clouds and are usually harmless.
what is a hurricane made of | how do hurricanes form | what fuels a hurricane => Hurricanes form over warm ocean water of at least about 26.5 °C. Warm, moist air rises, releases heat as clouds form, and the storm spins because of Earth's rotation.
hurricane vs typhoon vs cyclone | what is a typhoon | difference between hurricane and typhoon => They're the same kind of storm with different names: hurricane in the Atlantic and northeast Pacific, typhoon in the northwest Pacific, and cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
what is the saffir simpson scale | hurricane categories | what is a category 5 hurricane => The Saffir-Simpson scale rates hurricanes from Category 1 to 5 by wind speed. Category 5 means winds of 252 km/h (157 mph) or more.
what is the eye of a hurricane | why is the eye calm | eye wall => The eye is the calm, often clear center of a hurricane, usually 30 to 65 km wide. Around it is the eyewall, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
what is a storm surge | why is storm surge dangerous | hurricane flooding => A storm surge is seawater pushed onto land by a hurricane's winds. It causes most hurricane deaths and can raise the sea several meters.
when is hurricane season | atlantic hurricane season dates | when do hurricanes happen => The Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November, peaking in late August to September.
how are hurricanes named | who names hurricanes | hurricane name list => The World Meteorological Organization uses lists of names that repeat every six years. Names of very deadly or costly storms are retired.
which way do hurricanes spin | do hurricanes spin backwards in australia | coriolis effect hurricanes => Hurricanes spin counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere because of the Coriolis effect from Earth's rotation.
what is the coriolis effect | does the coriolis effect affect toilets | coriolis force => The Coriolis effect is how Earth's spin makes moving air and water curve, right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern. It's far too weak to decide which way a sink drains.
what is a sea breeze | land breeze vs sea breeze | why is it windy at the beach => On sunny days, land heats faster than the sea, so air rises over land and cool sea air blows in: a sea breeze. At night the land cools faster, making a land breeze toward the sea.
what are trade winds | what are the westerlies | global wind patterns => Trade winds blow steadily toward the equator from the east in the tropics. Farther from the equator, the westerlies blow from the west. Sailors used both to cross oceans.
what is the jet stream | how fast is the jet stream | why do flights east take less time => The jet stream is a fast, narrow river of wind about 9 to 12 km up, blowing west to east at often over 160 km/h. Flights going east ride it and arrive sooner.
what does an anemometer measure | how is wind speed measured | what is a wind vane => An anemometer measures wind speed, often with spinning cups. A wind vane points into the wind to show which direction it's coming from.
what is the beaufort scale | wind scale 0 to 12 | how strong is a gale => The Beaufort scale rates wind from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane force) by its effects, like moving leaves or big waves. A gale is force 8, about 62 to 74 km/h.
what is the windiest place on earth | strongest wind gust ever | fastest wind recorded => The strongest gust not from a tornado was 408 km/h on Barrow Island, Australia, during Cyclone Olivia in 1996. Commonwealth Bay in Antarctica is one of the windiest places overall.
what is wind chill | why does wind make it feel colder => Wind chill is how cold it feels on skin when wind blows heat away faster. A 0 °C day with strong wind can feel like minus 10 °C or colder.
why does humidity make it feel hotter | feels hotter than it is => The heat index combines temperature and humidity to show how hot it feels. Humid air slows sweat from evaporating, so your body cools less.
what causes heat waves | heat dome => A heat wave is a period of unusually hot weather lasting days. They often happen when a high pressure system, or heat dome, sits in place and traps heat.
what is a drought | what causes drought | drought effects => A drought is a long period with much less rain than normal. It dries up water supplies, harms crops and raises wildfire risk.
how do wildfires start | what causes forest fires | why are wildfires getting worse => Wildfires are started by lightning or people, through campfires, sparks, power lines or arson. Heat, drought and wind help them spread, and a warming climate makes fire seasons longer.
what is a monsoon | when is monsoon season | why do monsoons happen => A monsoon is a seasonal wind shift that brings a very rainy season, especially in South Asia from about June to September, when warm, wet air blows in from the ocean.
what is el nino | what is la nina | el nino effects => El Niño is when the tropical Pacific warms more than usual every few years, shifting weather around the world. La Niña is the opposite, with cooler Pacific water.
what is the polar vortex | polar vortex explained | why is it so cold polar vortex => The polar vortex is a big area of cold air swirling high above each pole. When it weakens or stretches, frigid Arctic air can spill south into places like the US and Europe.
how do meteorologists forecast weather | how are weather forecasts made | how accurate are weather forecasts => Forecasters use data from weather stations, balloons, radar and satellites, fed into supercomputer models that simulate the atmosphere. Forecasts are quite reliable for about 5 to 7 days.
what does a meteorologist do | how to become a meteorologist | weather scientist name => A meteorologist is a scientist who studies the atmosphere and forecasts weather. Most study physics, math and meteorology at university.
what is a weather balloon | how high do weather balloons go | radiosonde => A weather balloon carries a radiosonde, a small instrument that measures temperature, humidity, pressure and wind as it rises to about 30 km before bursting. Hundreds are launched worldwide twice a day.
what is doppler radar | how does weather radar work | radar for rain => Weather radar sends out radio waves that bounce off raindrops, snow and hail. Doppler radar also measures how they move, which helps spot rotation in storms.
what is a rain gauge | how is rainfall measured | how to make a rain gauge => A rain gauge collects rain to measure how much fell, in millimeters or inches. You can make one from a straight-sided clear jar with a ruler taped to it.
what is a thermometer | how does a thermometer work | who invented the thermometer => A thermometer measures temperature. Liquid ones work because the liquid expands as it warms and rises up a thin tube. Galileo made an early version around 1593.
what is the hottest temperature ever recorded | death valley temperature => The highest official air temperature is 56.7 °C (134 °F) at Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913, though some experts doubt that reading. Ground temperatures can get even hotter.
what is the coldest temperature ever recorded | vostok temperature => The coldest official air temperature is minus 89.2 °C, at Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983. Satellites have detected surface temperatures near minus 98 °C on the Antarctic plateau.
what is the wettest place on earth | rainiest place in the world | mawsynram => Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in northeast India are the wettest places, averaging around 11,000 to 12,000 mm of rain a year.
what is the driest place on earth | driest desert => The driest non-polar place is the Atacama Desert in Chile, where some weather stations have never recorded rain. The McMurdo Dry Valleys in Antarctica are even drier.
what is a desert | is antarctica a desert | largest desert in the world => A desert is any place that gets very little rain or snow, under about 250 mm a year. That makes Antarctica the largest desert on Earth, ahead of the Sahara.
what is the water cycle | how does the water cycle work | stages of the water cycle => The water cycle is how water moves around Earth: it evaporates, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation, and flows back to oceans, lakes and underground, over and over.
what is precipitation | types of precipitation | precipitation meaning => Precipitation is any water falling from clouds: rain, drizzle, snow, sleet, freezing rain or hail.
how do plants add water to air | evapotranspiration => Transpiration is when plants release water vapor through tiny holes in their leaves. A big tree can give off hundreds of liters a day.
what is runoff | what is infiltration | where does rain go => Runoff is rainwater that flows over the ground into streams and rivers. Infiltration is when it soaks into the soil and becomes groundwater.
is the water we drink old | how old is water | dinosaur water => Yes. Water keeps cycling, so the water you drink has been around for billions of years and may once have passed through a dinosaur or a cloud over an ancient ocean.
where is most of earths water | how much water is fresh | freshwater percentage => About 97% of Earth's water is salty ocean water. Only about 3% is fresh, and most of that is frozen in ice caps and glaciers or underground.
what is a rainbow made of | why do rainbows happen => Rainbows form when sunlight enters raindrops, bends, reflects off the back of the drop and bends again, splitting into colors. You see one with the Sun behind you and rain in front.
what is a double rainbow | why are double rainbow colors reversed | second rainbow => A double rainbow happens when light reflects twice inside the raindrops. The second bow is fainter and its colors are in reverse order.
what is a halo around the sun | 22 degree halo | ring around the moon => A halo is a ring of light around the Sun or Moon, made when light bends through ice crystals in thin, high cirrus clouds. A ring around the Moon can be a sign that rain is coming.
what are sun dogs | parhelion | bright spots next to the sun => Sun dogs are bright, colored spots on each side of the Sun, made when sunlight bends through flat, hexagon shaped ice crystals in the air.
why are sunsets red and orange | why is the sunset orange | sunset colors science => At sunset, sunlight travels through much more air. Blue light gets scattered away along the way, leaving reds and oranges to reach your eyes.
what is weather | what makes weather | weather definition => Weather is what the atmosphere is doing at a given time and place: temperature, wind, clouds, rain and humidity. It's driven by the Sun heating Earth unevenly.
what is a climate zone | types of climate zones | tropical temperate polar => Earth's main climate zones are tropical (hot near the equator), dry (deserts), temperate (mild with seasons), continental (cold winters, warm summers) and polar (cold all year).
what affects climate | factors that affect climate | why do places have different climates => Climate depends on distance from the equator, height above sea level, distance from the sea, ocean currents, winds and mountains.
why is it colder on mountains | why does temperature drop with height | why is the top of a mountain cold => Air gets thinner and expands as it rises, which makes it cool, about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 meters up on average. Thin air also holds less heat.
what is a rain shadow | why is one side of a mountain dry | rain shadow effect => When wet air hits a mountain, it rises, cools and drops its rain on that side. The air coming down the other side is dry, making a rain shadow.
what is a microclimate | urban heat island | why are cities hotter => A microclimate is a small area with a different climate from around it. Cities are often warmer than the countryside, called the urban heat island effect, because concrete and asphalt store heat.
what is the evidence for climate change | how do we know climate is changing | proof of global warming => Thermometers show the world has warmed about 1.3 °C since the late 1800s. Glaciers and Arctic sea ice are shrinking, sea level is rising, and spring comes earlier in many places.
what causes climate change now | is climate change caused by humans => Scientists agree that recent warming is mainly caused by humans burning coal, oil and gas, which adds carbon dioxide that traps heat. Cutting forests adds more.
how much carbon dioxide is in the air | co2 levels | keeling curve => Carbon dioxide has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to about 425 ppm as of 2025. The Keeling Curve has tracked it at Mauna Loa since 1958.
list of greenhouse gases | which gases trap heat => Greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere. The main ones are water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone.
why is methane bad for climate | where does methane come from => Methane (CH4) is a powerful greenhouse gas, trapping about 80 times more heat than CO2 over 20 years. It comes from cattle, rice fields, landfills, wetlands and gas leaks.
what is the paris agreement | paris climate agreement goal | 1.5 degrees => The Paris Agreement of 2015 is a deal among nearly all countries to keep warming well below 2 °C, and to try to limit it to 1.5 °C, above pre-industrial levels.
what is a carbon footprint | how to reduce your carbon footprint | carbon footprint meaning => A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gases caused by a person or activity. You can reduce yours by walking, biking, saving energy, wasting less food and reusing things.
what is sea level rise | why is sea level rising | how much has sea level risen => Sea level has risen about 20 to 25 cm since 1900, because warming water expands and melting glaciers and ice sheets add water. It's rising faster now, by over 4 mm a year.
what happens if all the ice melts | if antarctica melted => If all the ice on land melted, sea level would rise about 70 meters. That would take thousands of years, but even a meter or two would flood many coastal cities.
what is the difference between sea ice and land ice | does melting sea ice raise sea level | ice melting sea level => Sea ice already floats, so melting it barely changes sea level, like ice melting in a glass. Melting land ice, from glaciers and ice sheets, adds new water to the ocean and raises it.
what is the albedo effect | why does melting ice speed up warming | ice reflects sunlight => Albedo is how much sunlight a surface reflects. Ice reflects most sunlight, but dark ocean absorbs it. As ice melts, more heat is absorbed, which melts more ice.
what is a feedback loop in climate | positive feedback climate | climate tipping points => A feedback loop is when a change causes effects that make it bigger, like melting ice exposing dark water that warms more. Tipping points are where a change becomes hard to reverse.
types of renewable energy | renewable vs nonrenewable => Renewable energy comes from sources that don't run out, like sunlight, wind, flowing water, geothermal heat and plant fuels. Nonrenewable sources, like coal, oil and gas, take millions of years to form.
what are fossil fuels | how are fossil fuels formed | coal oil and gas => Fossil fuels are coal, oil and natural gas, formed over millions of years from buried remains of ancient plants and tiny sea creatures. Burning them releases carbon dioxide.
how does a wind turbine work | how do wind turbines make electricity | wind power => Wind turns the turbine's blades, which spin a shaft connected to a generator that makes electricity. Big turbines stand over 100 meters tall.
what is hydroelectric power | how do dams make electricity | hydropower => Hydroelectric dams let water flow down through turbines that spin generators. The Three Gorges Dam in China is the world's largest power station by capacity.
what is geothermal energy | how does geothermal power work | iceland geothermal => Geothermal energy uses heat from inside Earth. Hot water and steam from underground spin turbines or heat homes. Iceland gets most of its heating this way.
what is carbon capture | can we remove co2 from the air | direct air capture => Carbon capture traps carbon dioxide from power plants or straight from the air and stores it underground. It works but is still expensive and small compared to emissions.
what is ozone made of | ozone layer protects us => The ozone layer, in the stratosphere, is made of ozone (O3) molecules that absorb most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet rays.

what is smog | what causes smog | air pollution => Smog is a haze of air pollution. Modern smog forms when sunlight reacts with car and factory exhaust, making ground-level ozone, which is harmful to breathe.
what are the seasons caused by | why is it warmer in summer => Seasons happen because Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4 degrees. When your half of Earth tilts toward the Sun, sunlight is more direct and days are longer, so it's summer.
is earth closer to the sun in summer | why isnt summer caused by distance | perihelion and seasons => No. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter. Seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, not distance.
what is the equinox | when is the equinox => An equinox is when day and night are about equal everywhere, around 20 March and 22 or 23 September. The Sun is directly over the equator.
what is the midnight sun | why is it light all night in the arctic | polar night => Near the poles in summer, the Sun never sets for days or months, called the midnight sun. In winter, it never rises, called polar night.
