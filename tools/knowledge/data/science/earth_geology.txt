# tag: science.earth.geology
what is the crust | how thick is earths crust | earths crust facts => The crust is Earth's thin rocky outer layer. It's about 5 to 10 km thick under the oceans and about 30 to 50 km thick under continents, up to 70 km under big mountains.
what is the mantle | how thick is the mantle | what is the mantle made of => The mantle is the thick layer below the crust, about 2,900 km deep. It's hot solid rock that can flow very slowly, like extremely stiff putty.
what is the outer core | is the outer core liquid | outer core facts => The outer core is a layer of liquid iron and nickel about 2,200 km thick. Its swirling creates Earth's magnetic field.
what is the inner core | why is the inner core solid | inner core facts => The inner core is a solid ball of iron and nickel about 1,220 km in radius. It's as hot as the Sun's surface, but the huge pressure keeps it solid.
how far is it to the center of the earth | how deep is the center of the earth | radius of the earth => The center of the Earth is about 6,371 km below your feet on average.
what is the deepest hole ever dug | kola superdeep borehole | how deep have we drilled => The Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia reached about 12.2 km deep in 1989. That's still only a tiny fraction of the way through the crust.
what is the lithosphere | what is the asthenosphere | lithosphere vs asthenosphere => The lithosphere is the rigid outer shell: the crust plus the top of the mantle. It floats on the asthenosphere, a softer, slowly flowing layer of the upper mantle.
what is the moho | mohorovicic discontinuity | boundary between crust and mantle => The Moho is the boundary between the crust and the mantle. It was found in 1909 by Andrija Mohorovičić, who noticed earthquake waves speed up there.
what are tectonic plates | how many tectonic plates are there | what is plate tectonics => Earth's lithosphere is broken into about 15 major tectonic plates that slowly move. Plate tectonics explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountains and ocean trenches.
how fast do tectonic plates move | speed of plate movement | do continents move => Plates move about 2 to 10 cm a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.
what makes tectonic plates move | why do plates move | what drives plate tectonics => Plates are moved mostly by gravity pulling dense, cold plate edges down into the mantle (slab pull), helped by the push from spreading ridges and slow mantle movement.
what are the major tectonic plates | names of tectonic plates | biggest tectonic plate => Major plates include the Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian (sometimes split in two) and South American. The Pacific Plate is the biggest.
what is a divergent boundary | plates moving apart | what happens when plates pull apart => At a divergent boundary, plates pull apart and magma rises to make new crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge and Iceland are examples.
what is a convergent boundary | plates moving together | what happens when plates collide => At a convergent boundary, plates push together. One may sink under the other, making trenches and volcanoes, or both crumple up into mountains.
what is a transform boundary | plates sliding past each other | san andreas fault => At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other sideways. The San Andreas Fault in California is a famous example, and it causes earthquakes.
what is subduction | subduction zone | what happens in a subduction zone => Subduction is when a dense ocean plate sinks under another plate into the mantle. Subduction zones make deep trenches, big earthquakes and explosive volcanoes.
what is seafloor spreading | how does new ocean floor form | who discovered seafloor spreading => At mid-ocean ridges, magma rises and forms new seafloor that slowly spreads outward. Harry Hess proposed the idea in the early 1960s.
what is the mid atlantic ridge | longest mountain range underwater | mid ocean ridge => The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is an underwater mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, where the plates spread apart. Iceland sits on top of it.
when did pangaea break up | what was the supercontinent => Pangaea was a supercontinent that held almost all of Earth's land. It formed about 335 million years ago and started breaking apart about 200 million years ago.
what is the evidence for continental drift | how do we know continents moved | continents fit together like a puzzle => Evidence includes coastlines that fit together (like South America and Africa), matching fossils and rocks on different continents, and magnetic stripes on the seafloor.
will there be another supercontinent | future supercontinent | pangaea ultima => Probably. Scientists predict the continents will come together again in a few hundred million years, with names like Pangaea Proxima or Amasia.
how did the himalayas form | why are the himalayas so tall | india crashing into asia => The Himalayas formed when the Indian plate crashed into the Eurasian plate, starting about 50 million years ago. They're still rising a few millimeters a year.
how are mountains formed | types of mountains | how do mountains form => Most big mountains form when plates collide and crumple (fold mountains), but mountains also form from volcanoes, from blocks pushed up along faults, and from domes of rising magma.
what is a fault | what is a fault line | types of faults => A fault is a crack in Earth's crust where rocks have moved. Normal faults pull apart, reverse faults push together, and strike-slip faults slide sideways.
what is a rift valley | east african rift | how do rift valleys form => A rift valley forms where the crust is being pulled apart and a block drops down. The East African Rift is slowly splitting Africa and may one day form a new ocean.
what is an earthquake caused by | why do earthquakes happen | how do earthquakes start => Earthquakes happen when stress builds up along a fault until the rocks suddenly slip, sending out seismic waves that shake the ground.
what is the epicenter | what is the focus of an earthquake | epicenter vs hypocenter => The focus (hypocenter) is the spot underground where the rock first breaks. The epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
how are earthquakes measured | what is the richter scale | what is the moment magnitude scale => Earthquakes are measured by magnitude. Scientists now use the moment magnitude scale, which replaced the Richter scale. Each whole number up is about 32 times more energy.
what is a seismograph | how does a seismometer work | what records earthquakes => A seismometer detects ground shaking, and a seismograph records it as a wiggly line. Big wiggles mean strong shaking.
what was the biggest earthquake ever | strongest earthquake recorded | 1960 chile earthquake => The strongest earthquake ever recorded was the magnitude 9.5 Valdivia earthquake in Chile on 22 May 1960.
how many earthquakes happen each year | do earthquakes happen every day | earthquakes per year => Hundreds of thousands of earthquakes are detected each year, but most are too small to feel. Only about 15 to 20 a year reach magnitude 7 or more.
what are aftershocks | what is a foreshock | why do aftershocks happen => Aftershocks are smaller earthquakes after a big one, as the rocks settle. They can go on for weeks or years. Foreshocks are smaller quakes before a big one, but you can't know they're foreshocks until later.
can we predict earthquakes | can scientists predict when an earthquake will happen | earthquake prediction => No one can predict the exact time and place of an earthquake. Scientists can say which areas are likely to have big ones, and early warning systems can give seconds of notice.
what should you do in an earthquake | drop cover and hold on => Drop to your hands and knees, take cover under a sturdy table or desk, and hold on until the shaking stops. Stay away from windows, and don't run outside during shaking.
why do earthquakes cause so much damage | what makes earthquakes deadly | earthquake damage => Most deaths come from buildings collapsing. Earthquakes can also cause landslides, fires, tsunamis and liquefaction, where shaken wet ground acts like a liquid.
what is liquefaction | soil liquefaction | why do buildings sink in earthquakes => Liquefaction is when shaking makes wet, sandy soil behave like a liquid. Buildings on it can tilt or sink.
how are buildings made earthquake proof | earthquake resistant buildings | how do skyscrapers survive earthquakes => Earthquake resistant buildings use flexible steel frames, cross bracing, deep foundations and base isolators, like big rubber pads, that let the ground move under the building.
where do most earthquakes happen | earthquake zones | why does japan have so many earthquakes => Most earthquakes happen along plate boundaries, especially around the Pacific Ring of Fire. Japan sits where four plates meet, so it has lots of earthquakes.
how do volcanoes form | why do volcanoes exist | where do volcanoes come from => Volcanoes form where magma rises to the surface, usually at plate boundaries where plates pull apart or one sinks under another, or over hot spots in the mantle.
what is magma | magma vs lava | difference between magma and lava => Magma is melted rock underground. Once it erupts onto the surface, it's called lava.
what are the types of volcanoes | shield volcano | stratovolcano vs shield volcano => Shield volcanoes are wide with gentle slopes from runny lava, like Mauna Loa. Stratovolcanoes are steep cones of lava and ash with explosive eruptions, like Mount Fuji. Cinder cones are small and steep.
what is a hot spot volcano | how did hawaii form | hawaii hot spot => A hot spot is a plume of hot mantle rock that melts through a plate. As the Pacific Plate moves over the Hawaii hot spot, it built a chain of volcanic islands.
what is yellowstone | is yellowstone a supervolcano => Yellowstone sits on a hot spot and has had huge eruptions in the past, the last big one about 640,000 years ago. Scientists watch it closely and see no signs of a big eruption coming soon.
what is a supervolcano | biggest volcanic eruptions | what is a vei 8 => A supervolcano is one that has had an eruption throwing out over 1,000 cubic km of material, the top level (8) on the Volcanic Explosivity Index. Toba and Yellowstone are examples.
what is the biggest eruption in history | tambora 1815 => The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora in Indonesia was the biggest in recorded history. Its ash cooled the world, and 1816 became known as the year without a summer.
what happened to pompeii | mount vesuvius eruption | when did vesuvius erupt => In 79 AD, Mount Vesuvius in Italy erupted and buried the Roman towns of Pompeii and Herculaneum in ash and hot gas. The ash preserved the towns, which can still be visited.
what happened at mount st helens | mount st helens 1980 => On 18 May 1980, Mount St. Helens in Washington state erupted after its side collapsed in a huge landslide. It killed 57 people and blew off the top of the mountain.
what was krakatoa | krakatoa eruption 1883 | loudest sound ever => The 1883 eruption of Krakatoa in Indonesia was so loud it was heard about 4,800 km away, possibly the loudest sound in recorded history. It caused deadly tsunamis.
what is a pyroclastic flow | why are pyroclastic flows deadly | hot volcanic avalanche => A pyroclastic flow is a fast avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock rushing down a volcano at over 100 km/h. It's the deadliest volcanic hazard.
is volcanic ash dangerous | why does ash stop planes => Volcanic ash is tiny bits of rock and glass, not soft like wood ash. It can melt inside jet engines and stop them, so planes avoid ash clouds.
what is a lahar | volcanic mudflow | what is a lahar made of => A lahar is a volcanic mudflow of water, ash and rock that can rush down valleys like wet concrete. They can be very deadly.
what is an active volcano | dormant vs extinct volcano | active dormant extinct => An active volcano has erupted recently or shows signs it could. A dormant one hasn't erupted for a long time but might again. An extinct one isn't expected to ever erupt again.
how many active volcanoes are there | how many volcanoes are erupting | number of volcanoes on earth => There are about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes on land, and around 40 to 50 are erupting at any given time. Many more are hidden under the oceans.
what is the most active volcano | kilauea | most active volcano in the world => Kīlauea in Hawaii is one of the most active volcanoes in the world. Etna in Italy and Stromboli, nicknamed the Lighthouse of the Mediterranean, are also very active.
can you predict volcanic eruptions | how do scientists monitor volcanoes | volcano warning signs => Scientists watch for small earthquakes, the ground swelling, gas changes and heat. These often come before eruptions, so warnings are possible, though not exact.
what is a geyser | how do geysers work | old faithful => A geyser is a hot spring that shoots water and steam into the air. Underground water is heated by magma until it boils and blasts out. Old Faithful in Yellowstone erupts about every 60 to 110 minutes.
what is a hot spring | why are some springs hot | hot springs volcanic => A hot spring is groundwater heated by hot rock deep underground that flows up to the surface. Iceland and Japan have many.
what are the three rock types made from | igneous sedimentary metamorphic explained | rock types and how they form => Igneous rocks form from cooled magma or lava. Sedimentary rocks form from layers of bits pressed and cemented together. Metamorphic rocks form when rocks are changed by heat and pressure.
what is the rock cycle | how does the rock cycle work | rock cycle steps => The rock cycle is how rocks change from one type to another over millions of years: weathering and erosion, burial, heat and pressure, melting and cooling.
can a rock change into another rock | how does igneous become sedimentary | rock cycle example => Yes. Granite can weather into sand, which becomes sandstone, which heat and pressure can turn into quartzite. If it melts, it can cool into new igneous rock.
what is intrusive vs extrusive rock | why do some igneous rocks have big crystals | crystal size igneous => Intrusive igneous rocks cool slowly underground, so they have big crystals, like granite. Extrusive rocks cool quickly at the surface, so their crystals are tiny or absent, like basalt.
what is a mineral | mineral definition | rock vs mineral => A mineral is a natural, solid, non-living substance with a fixed chemical makeup and a crystal structure, like quartz. A rock is made of one or more minerals.
how many minerals are there | number of known minerals | how many types of minerals exist => Over 6,000 minerals are officially recognized, but only a few dozen are common in rocks.
what is the mohs scale | mohs hardness scale list | what is the hardness scale => The Mohs scale ranks mineral hardness from 1 to 10: talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, orthoclase, quartz, topaz, corundum and diamond. A harder mineral scratches a softer one.
how do you test a minerals hardness | scratch test minerals | what can scratch glass => Try scratching it with everyday things: a fingernail is about 2.5, a copper coin about 3.5, a steel knife or glass about 5.5. If the mineral scratches glass, it's harder than 5.5.
what is a streak test | mineral streak color | how to do a streak test => In a streak test, you rub a mineral across a rough white tile. The color of its powder, the streak, can identify it even when its outside color varies.
what is luster | mineral luster types | metallic vs non metallic luster => Luster is how a mineral reflects light. It can be metallic (like pyrite), glassy (like quartz), pearly, silky, greasy or dull.
what is cleavage in minerals | cleavage vs fracture | how minerals break => Cleavage is when a mineral breaks along flat, smooth surfaces, like mica peeling into sheets. Fracture is breaking unevenly, like quartz breaking with curved surfaces.
how do crystals form | why do crystals have shapes | how are crystals made => Crystals form when atoms line up in a repeating pattern as a liquid cools, a solution evaporates, or a gas cools. That repeating pattern gives crystals their flat faces.
how do you grow crystals at home | crystal growing experiment | how to make sugar crystals => Dissolve as much sugar, salt or alum as you can in hot water, then hang a string in it and leave it somewhere still. As it cools and evaporates over days, crystals grow on the string.
what is a geode | how do geodes form | what is inside a geode => A geode is a hollow rock lined with crystals. It forms when mineral-rich water seeps into a cavity and slowly deposits crystals, often quartz or amethyst, over a long time.
what is the biggest crystal ever found | giant crystal cave mexico | naica crystals => The Cave of the Crystals in Naica, Mexico, has gypsum crystals up to about 12 meters long, some of the largest natural crystals known.
what are gemstones | what makes a gem precious | precious vs semi precious stones => Gemstones are minerals or other materials cut and polished for jewelry. Diamond, ruby, sapphire and emerald are traditionally called precious stones.
what is an ore | what is ore mining | examples of ores => An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal or mineral to be worth mining. Hematite is an iron ore and bauxite is the main aluminium ore.
how is metal extracted from ore | what is smelting | how do you get iron from rock => Smelting heats ore with carbon (like coke) in a furnace, which pulls the oxygen away and leaves molten metal. Iron is made this way in a blast furnace.
what is weathering | types of weathering | physical vs chemical weathering => Weathering breaks rocks down where they are. Physical weathering cracks them by freezing, heat or roots. Chemical weathering changes their minerals, like acidic rain dissolving limestone.
what is erosion | weathering vs erosion | what causes erosion => Erosion is moving broken rock and soil to a new place, by water, wind, ice or gravity. Weathering breaks rock down, and erosion carries it away.
what is freeze thaw weathering | frost wedging | how does ice break rocks => Water seeps into cracks, freezes and expands by about 9%, pushing the crack wider. After many freezes and thaws, the rock splits apart.
what is deposition in geology | where does eroded sediment go | how are deltas formed => Deposition is when wind, water or ice drop the sediment they carry. Rivers slowing at the sea drop sediment and build deltas, like the Nile Delta.
how was the grand canyon formed | how old is the grand canyon | how deep is the grand canyon => The Grand Canyon was carved mostly by the Colorado River over about 5 to 6 million years, cutting through rock layers up to almost 2 billion years old. It's up to about 1.8 km deep.
how do caves form | how are limestone caves made | what are stalactites and stalagmites => Most caves form when slightly acidic rainwater dissolves limestone over thousands of years. Dripping water leaves minerals that build stalactites down from the ceiling and stalagmites up from the floor.
how do you remember stalactites vs stalagmites | which one hangs down => Stalactites hold tight to the ceiling (c for ceiling), and stalagmites might reach up from the ground (g for ground).
what is a landslide | what causes landslides | mudslide => A landslide is rock, soil or mud sliding down a slope. Heavy rain, earthquakes, volcanoes and cutting down trees can trigger them.
what is a sinkhole | how do sinkholes form | why does the ground collapse => A sinkhole is a hole that forms when the ground collapses into a space below, usually after water dissolves rock like limestone underground.
what are the layers of soil | soil horizons | topsoil subsoil bedrock => Soil has layers called horizons: a top layer of leaf litter, dark topsoil full of humus, lighter subsoil, broken rock, and then solid bedrock.
what are the types of soil | sand silt clay | best soil for plants => Soil types depend on grain size: sand (big, drains fast), silt (medium) and clay (tiny, holds water). Loam is a mix of all three and is best for most plants.
types of fossils | trace fossils => Fossils are remains or traces of ancient life. Body fossils include bones, teeth and shells, often turned to stone. Trace fossils include footprints, burrows and dung.
why are fossils usually in sedimentary rock | where are fossils found | why arent fossils in igneous rock => Fossils form when remains are quickly buried in sediment like mud or sand, which later hardens into rock. Igneous rock forms from hot magma, which would destroy remains.
how do insects get stuck in amber | amber fossils => Amber is fossilized tree resin. Insects, feathers and even small lizards got trapped in the sticky resin, which hardened and preserved them for millions of years.
how do scientists know how old rocks are | radiometric dating | how old is the oldest rock => Scientists measure radioactive elements in rocks that decay at known rates, like uranium turning into lead. This is called radiometric dating.
what is the law of superposition | which rock layers are oldest | rock layers age => The law of superposition says that in undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, the oldest layers are at the bottom and the youngest at the top.
what is the geologic time scale | eons eras periods | geologic time explained => The geologic time scale splits Earth's 4.5 billion year history into eons, eras, periods and epochs, based on rocks and fossils. We live in the Cenozoic Era.
what are the eras of earths history | paleozoic mesozoic cenozoic | what era were dinosaurs in => The last three eras are the Paleozoic (about 539 to 252 million years ago), the Mesozoic or Age of Dinosaurs (252 to 66 million years ago) and the Cenozoic, the age of mammals, since then.
what were the periods of the dinosaurs | triassic jurassic cretaceous | when was the jurassic period => The Mesozoic has three periods: the Triassic (about 252 to 201 million years ago), the Jurassic (201 to 145 million years ago) and the Cretaceous (145 to 66 million years ago).
what is the precambrian | how long was the precambrian | precambrian time => The Precambrian is all of Earth's history before about 539 million years ago, almost 90% of Earth's history. Life was mostly microscopic for most of it.
when did complex life appear | cambrian period => The Cambrian explosion, starting about 539 million years ago, is when most major groups of animals quickly appear in the fossil record, over roughly 20 million years.
what is the anthropocene | are we in a new geological age | human age geology => The Anthropocene is a proposed name for the time when humans have greatly changed Earth. In 2024, geologists voted not to make it an official unit of the geologic time scale.
what was the ice age | how cold was the ice age => The last glacial period peaked about 20,000 years ago, when ice sheets covered much of North America and Europe and sea level was about 120 meters lower. It ended about 11,700 years ago.
are we in an ice age now | is earth in an ice age | interglacial period => Technically yes. Earth is in an ice age that started about 2.6 million years ago because there are still ice sheets at the poles. We're in a warmer interglacial period within it.
what was snowball earth | was the whole earth frozen | snowball earth theory => Snowball Earth is the idea that Earth was almost entirely covered in ice at least twice, around 700 million and 650 million years ago.
how did earth form | earth formation => Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago from dust and rocks orbiting the young Sun. They clumped together, and the young planet got hot enough for heavy iron to sink to the core.
where did earths water come from | how did earth get its oceans | origin of water on earth => Earth's water probably came from water locked in the rocks it formed from, plus some delivered by asteroids and comets. Scientists are still debating the mix.
what is a mineral vein | what are veins in rocks | how do quartz veins form => A vein is a sheet of minerals that filled a crack in rock. Hot water carrying dissolved minerals flowed through the crack and left them behind, sometimes including gold.
what is a meteorite crater | how do impact craters form | biggest crater on earth => Impact craters form when asteroids or comets hit the surface at high speed. The biggest known on Earth is the Vredefort crater in South Africa, originally about 170 to 300 km wide.
what is meteor crater arizona | barringer crater | crater in arizona => Meteor Crater (Barringer Crater) in Arizona is about 1.2 km wide. It was made about 50,000 years ago by an iron meteorite about 50 meters across.
what is geology | what does a geologist do | how to become a geologist => Geology is the study of Earth, its rocks, minerals, landforms and history. Geologists find resources, study earthquakes and volcanoes, and help keep buildings safe.
what is a seismologist | what is a volcanologist | earth scientist jobs => A seismologist studies earthquakes, a volcanologist studies volcanoes, and a paleontologist studies fossils. They're all kinds of earth scientists.
what is the continental shelf | continental shelf depth | what is the continental slope => The continental shelf is the shallow, gently sloping edge of a continent under the sea, usually less than about 200 m deep. It ends at the steeper continental slope down to the deep ocean.
what is the tallest mountain from base to peak | is mauna kea taller than everest => Mauna Kea in Hawaii is over 10,000 meters from its base on the ocean floor to its peak, taller than Everest, though only about 4,207 meters rises above sea level.
how tall is mount everest | everest height | how high is everest in feet => Mount Everest is 8,848.86 meters (29,032 feet) above sea level, measured by China and Nepal in 2020. It's still growing very slowly.
what is the farthest point from earths center | chimborazo | highest point from the center of earth => The summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador is the farthest point from Earth's center, because Earth bulges at the equator.
is the earth a perfect sphere | why does earth bulge | shape of the earth => No. Earth is slightly squashed, bulging at the equator because of its spin. It's about 43 km wider across the equator than from pole to pole.

what is a delta | what is an estuary | river mouth landforms => A delta is a fan of land made where a river drops sediment as it meets the sea. An estuary is where a river's fresh water mixes with salty seawater.
how do rivers form | parts of a river | source and mouth of a river => Rivers form from rain, melting snow and springs gathering in streams. A river starts at its source, flows downhill through its course, and ends at its mouth.

what is a water table | water table meaning | how deep is groundwater => The water table is the top of the zone where the ground is soaked with water. It rises after rain and falls in dry times.
