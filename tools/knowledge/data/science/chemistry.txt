# tag: science.chem.matter
what is matter | what is matter made of | matter definition science => Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Everything you can touch, plus air and water, is matter, and it's all made of atoms.
what is an element | element definition chemistry | what makes something an element => An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, like oxygen, gold or carbon. It can't be broken into simpler substances by chemical reactions.
what is a compound | compound definition | element vs compound => A compound is a substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio, like water (H2O) or salt (NaCl).
what is a mixture | mixture definition | compound vs mixture => A mixture is two or more substances mixed but not chemically joined, like salad, sand and water, or air. You can usually separate them by physical methods.
what is a pure substance | pure vs impure | what does pure mean in chemistry => In chemistry, a pure substance is made of only one element or one compound. Pure water is only H2O, while tap water has dissolved minerals in it.
what is a homogeneous mixture | heterogeneous mixture | homogeneous vs heterogeneous => A homogeneous mixture looks the same all the way through, like salt water or air. A heterogeneous mixture has parts you can see, like cereal in milk or sand in water.
what is a solution in chemistry | solute solvent solution | what is a solute => A solution is a mixture where one substance dissolves completely in another. In salt water, salt is the solute, water is the solvent, and together they're the solution.
why is water called the universal solvent | what dissolves in water | why does water dissolve so much => Water dissolves more substances than almost any other liquid, because its molecules have slightly charged ends that pull apart other molecules and ions.
what is solubility | what does soluble mean | soluble vs insoluble => Soluble means a substance can dissolve, like sugar in water. Insoluble means it can't, like sand. Solubility is how much can dissolve in a certain amount of solvent.
what is a saturated solution | when does a solution become saturated | supersaturated solution => A saturated solution holds as much solute as it can at that temperature. Add more and it just sits at the bottom. Heating usually lets more dissolve.
how can you make sugar dissolve faster | what makes things dissolve faster | speed up dissolving => Things dissolve faster if you stir, use hotter water, or break the solid into smaller pieces so more surface touches the water.
what is a suspension | what is a colloid | examples of colloids => In a suspension, bits float in a liquid and settle over time, like muddy water. In a colloid, tiny bits stay spread out and don't settle, like milk, fog or jelly.
what is concentration | dilute vs concentrated | how do you calculate concentration => Concentration is how much solute is in a certain amount of solution. A concentrated drink has lots of flavor in it, and a dilute one has been watered down.
how do you separate mixtures | ways to separate mixtures | separation techniques => Mixtures can be separated by filtering, evaporating, distilling, using a magnet, decanting (pouring off) or chromatography, depending on what's in them.
what is filtration | how does filtering work | what can a filter separate => Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid by pouring it through filter paper. The liquid (filtrate) passes through and the solid (residue) stays behind.
how do you separate salt and water | how to get salt out of salt water | evaporating salt water => Heat the salt water so the water evaporates, leaving salt crystals behind. To keep the water too, use distillation.
how do you separate sand and salt | sand and salt experiment | separating sand salt and water => Add water to dissolve the salt, filter out the sand, then evaporate the water to get the salt back.
what is distillation | how does distillation work | what is distilled water => Distillation boils a liquid and then cools the vapor back into liquid in a separate container. It leaves dissolved solids behind, which is how distilled water is made.
what is fractional distillation | how is crude oil separated | fractional distillation of oil => Fractional distillation separates liquids with different boiling points. Crude oil is heated and its parts, like gasoline, diesel and kerosene, condense at different heights in a tall column.
what is chromatography | how does paper chromatography work | separating ink colors => Paper chromatography separates dyes. A water front climbs up the paper, carrying each dye a different distance, so a black ink can split into blue, red and yellow.
what is a physical property | examples of physical properties | physical properties of matter => Physical properties can be observed without changing what a substance is, like color, density, hardness, melting point and whether it's magnetic.
what is a chemical property | examples of chemical properties | chemical vs physical properties => A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to become something new, like whether it burns, rusts or reacts with acid.
what is a physical change | examples of physical changes | is melting a physical change => A physical change alters the form of a substance but not what it is. Melting ice, tearing paper and dissolving sugar are physical changes and often can be reversed.
what is a chemical change | examples of chemical changes | signs of a chemical change => A chemical change makes new substances. Signs include a color change, gas bubbles, light or heat being given off, a new smell, or a solid forming. Burning, rusting and baking are examples.
is cooking an egg a chemical change | is boiling water a chemical change | physical or chemical change examples => Cooking an egg is a chemical change, because heat permanently changes its proteins. Boiling water is a physical change, because steam is still water.
is burning a chemical change | is rusting a chemical change | is dissolving a chemical change => Burning and rusting are chemical changes, because they make new substances. Dissolving sugar is a physical change, since you can get the sugar back by evaporating the water.
what are the properties of metals | what are metals like | characteristics of metals => Metals are usually shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable (can be hammered into shapes), ductile (can be pulled into wires) and solid at room temperature.
what are the properties of nonmetals | what are nonmetals like | characteristics of nonmetals => Nonmetals are usually dull, brittle when solid, and poor conductors of heat and electricity. Many are gases at room temperature, like oxygen and nitrogen.
what are metalloids | which elements are metalloids | properties of metalloids => Metalloids have properties between metals and nonmetals. The usual ones are boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. Silicon's semiconductor behavior makes it great for chips.
what does malleable mean | what does ductile mean | malleable vs ductile => Malleable means a material can be hammered or pressed into shapes without breaking, like gold foil. Ductile means it can be stretched into a wire, like copper.
brittle materials | is glass brittle => Brittle means a material breaks or shatters instead of bending. Glass, chalk and solid sulfur are brittle.
what is an alloy | examples of alloys | why do we make alloys => An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements. Alloys are often stronger or more useful than pure metals. Steel, brass, bronze and solder are alloys.
what is steel made of | how is steel made | iron vs steel => Steel is mostly iron with a little carbon, usually under 2%. The carbon makes it much stronger and harder than pure iron.
what is stainless steel | why doesnt stainless steel rust | stainless steel made of => Stainless steel is steel with at least about 10.5% chromium. The chromium forms a thin invisible oxide layer that protects it from rusting.
what is brass made of | brass vs bronze => Brass is copper mixed with zinc, and it's used in instruments and door handles. Bronze is mostly copper mixed with tin, and it was used for tools and statues.
what is 24 karat gold | what does karat mean | 18k vs 24k gold => Karats measure gold purity: 24 karat is pure gold. 18 karat is 75% gold, and 14 karat is about 58% gold, mixed with other metals to make it harder.
what is solder made of | why is solder used | lead free solder => Solder is a low melting point alloy used to join electronic parts. It used to be tin and lead, but most solder now is lead free, mostly tin with some silver and copper.
# tag: science.chem.bonding
what is a chemical bond | how do atoms stick together | types of chemical bonds => A chemical bond is a force holding atoms together. The main types are ionic bonds (atoms swap electrons), covalent bonds (atoms share electrons) and metallic bonds.
what is an ionic bond | how do ionic bonds form | ionic bonding example => An ionic bond forms when one atom gives electrons to another, making a positive ion and a negative ion that attract. In table salt, sodium gives an electron to chlorine.
what is a covalent bond | how do covalent bonds form | covalent bonding example => A covalent bond forms when two atoms share a pair of electrons. Water is held together by covalent bonds between oxygen and hydrogen atoms.
what is a metallic bond | how are metals bonded | sea of electrons => In a metallic bond, metal atoms share a sea of free electrons that move around them. That's why metals conduct electricity and can bend without breaking.
ionic vs covalent | difference between ionic and covalent bonds | how to tell if a bond is ionic or covalent => Ionic bonds usually form between a metal and a nonmetal, by transferring electrons. Covalent bonds usually form between nonmetals, by sharing electrons.
what are properties of ionic compounds | why does salt have a high melting point | ionic compounds conduct electricity => Ionic compounds usually have high melting points, form crystals, and conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water, because their ions can move.
what is a double bond | what is a triple bond | single double triple bonds => A single bond shares one pair of electrons, a double bond shares two pairs, and a triple bond shares three pairs. Nitrogen gas has a very strong triple bond.
what is a diatomic molecule | which elements are diatomic | list of diatomic elements => Diatomic elements exist as pairs of atoms. The seven are hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2).
what is a hydrogen bond | why does water stick together | hydrogen bonding water => A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen in one molecule and a slightly negative atom like oxygen in another. It's why water has surface tension and a high boiling point.
what is a polar molecule | is water polar | polar vs nonpolar => A polar molecule has a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end. Water is polar, which is why it dissolves salt. Oil is nonpolar, so it doesn't mix with water.
why dont oil and water mix | why does oil float on water => Water molecules are polar and stick to each other, while oil molecules are nonpolar. The oil gets pushed out, and since it's less dense, it floats on top.
how does soap work | why does soap remove grease | how does soap clean => Soap molecules have one end that likes water and one end that likes oil. They surround grease, with oily ends inside and watery ends outside, so it rinses away.
what is a chemical formula | how do you read a chemical formula | what do the small numbers in formulas mean => A chemical formula shows which atoms are in a substance and how many. In H2O, the small 2 means two hydrogen atoms, and O with no number means one oxygen.
what is valency | what is valence | how do you find valency => Valency is how many bonds an atom usually makes. Hydrogen has a valency of 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3 and carbon 4.
what are allotropes | allotropes of carbon | what is an allotrope => Allotropes are different forms of the same element. Diamond, graphite, graphene and buckyballs are all pure carbon, with atoms arranged differently.
why is diamond hard but graphite soft | diamond vs graphite | both made of carbon => In diamond, each carbon atom bonds to four others in a strong 3D network. In graphite, atoms form flat layers that slide over each other, so it's soft and good for pencils.
what is graphene | why is graphene special | graphene discovery => Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb pattern. It's incredibly strong, light and conducts electricity. Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov won a Nobel Prize for it in 2010.
what is a buckyball | what is buckminsterfullerene | c60 molecule => A buckyball is a molecule of 60 carbon atoms shaped like a soccer ball. It was discovered in 1985 and named after the architect Buckminster Fuller.
is pencil lead made of lead | what is in a pencil | pencil graphite => No. Pencil lead is graphite mixed with clay, not the metal lead. People once thought graphite was a type of lead.
# tag: science.chem.reactions
what are reactants and products | reactants vs products | what is a product in chemistry => Reactants are the substances you start with in a chemical reaction. Products are the new substances made. In an equation, reactants are on the left and products on the right.
what is a chemical equation | how do you read a chemical equation | word equation => A chemical equation shows a reaction using formulas. For example, 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O means hydrogen and oxygen react to make water.
how do you balance chemical equations | balancing equations steps => Balancing means making sure there are the same number of each type of atom on both sides. You change the big numbers in front of formulas, never the small ones inside.
what is the law of conservation of mass | does mass change in a reaction | conservation of mass => In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, so the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.
why does burnt wood weigh less | where does the mass go when something burns | ash weighs less than wood => The missing mass goes into the air as carbon dioxide and water vapor. If you caught all the gases, the total mass would be the same as before.
what is combustion | what is burning in chemistry | combustion reaction => Combustion is a reaction where a fuel reacts quickly with oxygen, giving off heat and light. Burning methane makes carbon dioxide and water.
what is the fire triangle | what does fire need to burn | three things fire needs => Fire needs three things: fuel, heat and oxygen. Remove any one, and the fire goes out. That's how fire extinguishers work.
types of fire extinguishers | why not use water on grease fires => Extinguishers remove heat or oxygen. Water cools, foam and carbon dioxide smother. Never use water on oil or grease fires, because it can make burning oil splash and spread.
what is complete vs incomplete combustion | what makes carbon monoxide | incomplete combustion => Complete combustion has plenty of oxygen and makes carbon dioxide and water. Incomplete combustion lacks oxygen and makes poisonous carbon monoxide and soot.
what is oxidation | what is reduction | redox reaction => Oxidation is when a substance gains oxygen or loses electrons. Reduction is when it loses oxygen or gains electrons. They always happen together in redox reactions.
what is rusting | what causes rust | how does iron rust => Rusting is iron reacting with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron oxide. It needs both air and water, and salt speeds it up.
how do you stop rust | how to prevent rusting | ways to protect iron from rust => You can prevent rust by painting, oiling, coating with plastic, galvanizing with zinc, or using stainless steel. All of these keep air and water off the iron.
what is galvanizing | why is zinc used to stop rust | sacrificial protection => Galvanizing coats iron or steel with zinc. Zinc reacts more easily than iron, so even if scratched, the zinc corrodes first and protects the iron.
what is corrosion | is tarnish corrosion | why does silver tarnish => Corrosion is a metal slowly being eaten away by reactions with its surroundings. Silver tarnishes black when it reacts with sulfur compounds in the air.
why does copper turn green | what is patina => Copper reacts slowly with air, water and carbon dioxide, forming a green coating called patina. The Statue of Liberty is copper and turned green over about 30 years.
what is an exothermic reaction | examples of exothermic reactions | reactions that release heat => An exothermic reaction gives off heat, so its surroundings get warmer. Burning, hand warmers, and neutralization are exothermic.
what is an endothermic reaction | examples of endothermic reactions | reactions that absorb heat => An endothermic reaction takes in heat, so its surroundings get colder. Instant cold packs, baking soda with vinegar, and photosynthesis are endothermic.
how do instant cold packs work | why do ice packs get cold when you squeeze them | cold pack chemistry => Squeezing an instant cold pack breaks an inner bag so water mixes with a chemical like ammonium nitrate. Dissolving it takes in heat, making the pack cold.
how do hand warmers work | why do hand warmers get hot | hand warmer chemistry => Air activated hand warmers contain iron powder that rusts very fast when exposed to air. Rusting gives off heat, keeping your hands warm for hours.
what are the types of chemical reactions | synthesis decomposition reactions | main reaction types => Main reaction types include synthesis (things combine), decomposition (one thing breaks apart), single replacement, double replacement and combustion.
what is a decomposition reaction | example of decomposition | thermal decomposition => In decomposition, one substance breaks down into simpler ones. Heating calcium carbonate splits it into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide.
what is a displacement reaction | reactivity series displacement | single replacement example => In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element pushes a less reactive one out of a compound. An iron nail in copper sulfate solution gets coated in copper.
what is the reactivity series | reactivity series order | metal reactivity list => The reactivity series ranks metals by how easily they react. A common order is potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, copper, silver and gold.
why is gold not reactive | why doesnt gold rust | why is gold found pure in nature => Gold is one of the least reactive metals, so it doesn't rust or tarnish and is often found as pure nuggets in nature.
what is a precipitate | precipitation reaction chemistry | what does precipitate mean => A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. It makes the liquid cloudy and settles to the bottom.
what is a catalyst | how do catalysts work | catalyst examples => A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzymes in your body and the catalytic converter in a car are catalysts.
what is a catalytic converter | why do cars have catalytic converters | catalytic converter metals => A catalytic converter uses metals like platinum, palladium and rhodium to turn harmful exhaust gases into less harmful ones like carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water.
what affects the rate of a reaction | how to speed up a reaction | reaction rate factors => Reactions go faster with higher temperature, higher concentration, more surface area (smaller pieces), higher pressure for gases, or a catalyst.
why does food go bad faster in warm places | why do fridges slow down spoiling | temperature and food spoiling => Warmth speeds up the reactions and microbe growth that spoil food. A fridge slows them down, so food lasts longer.
what is activation energy | why do reactions need a spark | what starts a reaction => Activation energy is the energy needed to get a reaction started. A match gives the activation energy for wood to burn.
what is a reversible reaction | can chemical reactions be reversed | reversible reaction example => Some reactions can go both ways. Heating blue copper sulfate crystals drives off water and turns them white, and adding water turns them blue again.
what happens when you mix baking soda and vinegar | baking soda and vinegar reaction | why does vinegar and baking soda fizz => Baking soda (a base) reacts with vinegar (an acid) to make carbon dioxide gas, water and sodium acetate. The bubbles are the carbon dioxide.
what is the mentos and diet coke reaction | mentos geyser => It's mostly physical, not chemical. The rough surface of the Mentos gives carbon dioxide in the soda lots of spots to form bubbles all at once, shooting a foamy geyser out.
what is elephant toothpaste | how does elephant toothpaste work | elephant toothpaste experiment => Elephant toothpaste is a foam made when hydrogen peroxide breaks down quickly into water and oxygen, sped up by yeast or potassium iodide. Dish soap traps the oxygen into a big foamy tower. Wear goggles.
what makes firework colors | firework chemistry => Firework colors come from metal compounds: strontium makes red, sodium yellow, barium green, copper blue, and calcium orange. Heat makes the atoms give off their own color of light.
what is a flame test | how does a flame test work | flame test colors => In a flame test, you put a metal compound in a flame and look at the color. Lithium gives red, sodium orange-yellow, potassium lilac, and copper blue-green.
why do cut apples go brown | stop apples browning => When you cut an apple, an enzyme reacts with oxygen and turns compounds in the apple brown. Lemon juice slows it down because its acid and vitamin C block the reaction.
why does toast turn brown | why does cooked meat brown => The Maillard reaction is a reaction between sugars and proteins when food is heated, making it brown and tasty. It happens in toast, seared meat, cookies and roasted coffee.
how does sugar turn into caramel | caramel chemistry => Caramelization is sugar breaking down when heated to around 160 °C or more, turning brown and making new flavors.
what is electrolysis | how does electrolysis work | electrolysis of water => Electrolysis uses electricity to split a compound. Running current through water splits it into hydrogen gas at one electrode and oxygen gas at the other.
what is electroplating | how do you electroplate | how is gold plating done => Electroplating uses electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of metal, like silver or gold. It makes things look nicer or protects them from rust.
what is a polymer | examples of polymers | what does polymer mean => A polymer is a giant molecule made of many small units, called monomers, joined in long chains. Plastics, rubber, DNA, wool and starch are all polymers.
how is plastic made | what is plastic made from | plastic chemistry => Most plastics are made from chemicals taken from oil and natural gas. Small molecules like ethylene are joined into long polymer chains, like polyethylene.
why is plastic bad for the environment | plastic pollution => Most plastics don't rot. They can last hundreds of years, breaking into tiny microplastics that end up in oceans, animals and even us.
plastic recycling codes | what is number 1 plastic => The number in the recycling triangle shows the plastic type. 1 is PET (drink bottles), 2 is HDPE (milk jugs), 3 is PVC, 4 is LDPE (bags), 5 is polypropylene, 6 is polystyrene and 7 is other.
what is biodegradable | what does biodegradable mean | biodegradable vs compostable => Biodegradable means microbes can break it down into natural substances over time. Compostable means it breaks down into compost under the right conditions, usually faster.
what is organic chemistry | what are organic compounds | why is carbon special => Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Carbon can form four bonds and long chains and rings, so it makes millions of compounds, including all the molecules of life.
what are hydrocarbons | examples of hydrocarbons | what is a hydrocarbon => Hydrocarbons are compounds made only of hydrogen and carbon. Methane, propane, octane and the other parts of crude oil are hydrocarbons.
what are alkanes | alkanes list | methane ethane propane butane => Alkanes are hydrocarbons with only single bonds. The first four are methane (1 carbon), ethane (2), propane (3) and butane (4).
# tag: science.chem.acids
what are acids like | properties of acids | examples of acids => Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red, have a pH below 7 and react with metals and bases. Lemon juice, vinegar and stomach acid are acids.
what are bases like | properties of bases | examples of bases => Bases taste bitter, feel slippery, turn red litmus blue and have a pH above 7. Soap, baking soda, bleach and ammonia are bases.
what is an alkali | alkali vs base | what is the difference between a base and an alkali => An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, like sodium hydroxide. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
what is the ph scale | what does the ph scale go from | ph scale range => The pH scale usually runs from 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral and above 7 is basic (alkaline). Each whole step means a 10 times difference in acidity.
what is neutral ph | what has a ph of 7 | is water neutral => A pH of 7 is neutral, neither acidic nor basic. Pure water at 25 °C has a pH of 7.
what is the ph of lemon juice | what is the ph of vinegar | ph of common things => Lemon juice is about pH 2, vinegar about 2.5 to 3, cola about 2.5, coffee about 5, milk about 6.5, blood about 7.4, baking soda solution about 8 to 9, and bleach about 12 to 13.
what is the ph of stomach acid | how acidic is your stomach | stomach ph => Stomach acid has a pH of about 1.5 to 3.5. It's mostly hydrochloric acid, which helps break down food and kill germs.
what is the ph of blood | why does blood ph matter | normal blood ph => Normal blood pH is about 7.35 to 7.45, slightly basic. Your body works hard to keep it in that narrow range.
what is the strongest acid | most acidic thing | strongest acid in the world => Some of the strongest acids are superacids like fluoroantimonic acid, which is far stronger than sulfuric acid. In school, strong acids include hydrochloric, sulfuric and nitric acid.
strong acid vs weak acid | what makes an acid strong | strong vs concentrated acid => A strong acid splits completely into ions in water, like hydrochloric acid. A weak acid only partly splits, like vinegar. Strong isn't the same as concentrated, which means how much is dissolved.
what is neutralization | what happens when an acid and base mix | acid plus base equals => Neutralization is an acid reacting with a base to make a salt and water. For example, hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide make sodium chloride and water.
why do antacids work | how do antacids stop heartburn | what is in antacid tablets => Antacids contain bases like calcium carbonate or magnesium hydroxide. They neutralize extra stomach acid, easing heartburn.
how do you treat a bee sting and wasp sting | bee sting acid or base | wasp sting vinegar myth => The old advice is baking soda for bee stings and vinegar for wasp stings, but it doesn't really work, since stings are complex mixtures injected under the skin. Remove a bee stinger and use a cold pack.
what is an indicator | what does an indicator do in chemistry | ph indicator examples => An indicator changes color depending on whether something is acidic or basic. Examples are litmus paper, universal indicator and phenolphthalein.
what is litmus paper | how does litmus paper work | red and blue litmus => Litmus paper is made with a dye from lichens. Acids turn blue litmus red, and bases turn red litmus blue.
what is universal indicator | universal indicator colors | ph color chart => Universal indicator changes through a rainbow of colors to show the pH: red for strong acids, orange and yellow for weak acids, green for neutral, and blue to purple for bases.
what is phenolphthalein | phenolphthalein color | pink indicator => Phenolphthalein is colorless in acids and neutral solutions and turns pink in bases. It's often used in titrations.
how do you make red cabbage indicator | red cabbage ph experiment | cabbage juice indicator => Chop red cabbage, soak it in hot water and strain it to get purple juice. Acids turn it pink or red and bases turn it blue, green or yellow.
what is acid rain | what causes acid rain | effects of acid rain => Acid rain forms when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels mix with water in clouds. It can harm forests, lakes and stone buildings.
what is ocean acidification | why is the ocean becoming acidic | ocean acidification effects => The ocean absorbs carbon dioxide from the air, which forms carbonic acid and lowers its pH. This makes it harder for corals and shellfish to build their shells.
what is a salt in chemistry | is salt only table salt | examples of salts => In chemistry, a salt is any ionic compound made when an acid reacts with a base. Table salt is just one example, others include calcium carbonate and copper sulfate.
what is titration | how does titration work | titration experiment => Titration finds the exact concentration of an acid or base by slowly adding one to the other until an indicator changes color, showing it's just neutralized.
what does ph stand for | what is the p in ph | ph meaning letters => The letters pH are often said to stand for power of hydrogen or potential of hydrogen. It measures the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution.
is soap acidic or basic | why does soap feel slippery | soap ph => Most bar soaps are basic, with a pH around 9 to 10. Bases react with oils on your skin, which is part of why soapy water feels slippery.
what is the ph of soil | why does soil ph matter | hydrangea color soil => Most plants grow best in soil with a pH between about 6 and 7.5. Some flowers, like hydrangeas, turn blue in acidic soil and pink in basic soil.
# tag: science.chem.periodic-table
how is the periodic table arranged | how are elements ordered | why is the periodic table that shape => The periodic table orders elements by atomic number. Elements with similar properties line up in columns called groups, and the rows are called periods.
what are groups on the periodic table | what are columns called | what do groups mean => Groups are the 18 vertical columns. Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, so they react in similar ways.
what are periods on the periodic table | what are rows called | how many periods are there => Periods are the 7 horizontal rows. All elements in a period have the same number of electron shells.
what are the alkali metals | group 1 elements | why are alkali metals so reactive => The alkali metals are group 1: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium. They have one outer electron that they lose easily, so they react violently with water.
what happens when you put sodium in water | sodium and water reaction | potassium in water => Sodium fizzes and zooms around on water, making hydrogen gas and sodium hydroxide. Potassium reacts so strongly the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame. Never try this at home.
why are alkali metals stored in oil | how do you store sodium | why is sodium kept in oil => Alkali metals react with oxygen and water vapor in the air, so they're stored under oil to keep air and moisture away.
what are the alkaline earth metals | group 2 elements | alkaline earth metals list => The alkaline earth metals are group 2: beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium. They're reactive but less so than group 1.
what are the halogens | group 17 elements | what does halogen mean => The halogens are group 17: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine and tennessine. They're very reactive nonmetals, and halogen means salt maker.
what are the noble gases | group 18 elements | why are noble gases unreactive => The noble gases are group 18: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon and oganesson. Their outer shells are full, so they hardly react with anything.
what are transition metals | where are the transition metals | properties of transition metals => Transition metals are the big block in the middle of the table, groups 3 to 12. They include iron, copper, gold and silver. Many are strong, dense and form colorful compounds.
what are the lanthanides and actinides | why are two rows at the bottom of the periodic table | what are the f block elements => The lanthanides and actinides are the two rows usually shown below the main table. They really belong in periods 6 and 7, but are moved down to keep the table from being too wide.
what are rare earth elements | why are rare earth metals important | rare earths list => Rare earth elements are the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. They're used in magnets, phones, wind turbines and electric cars. Despite the name, many aren't actually that rare.
what is the most reactive metal | most reactive element | which element reacts the most => Caesium and francium are the most reactive metals, though francium is so rare and radioactive that caesium is the one used. Fluorine is the most reactive nonmetal.
what is the lightest element | lightest metal | lightest gas => Hydrogen is the lightest element. Lithium is the lightest metal, light enough to float on oil.
what is the heaviest element | heaviest naturally occurring element | densest element => Oganesson (118) has the most protons. The heaviest element found in large amounts in nature is uranium. The densest element is osmium.
which elements are liquid at room temperature | what elements are liquids => Only two elements are liquid at normal room temperature: mercury and bromine. Gallium and caesium melt just above it, around 28 to 30 °C.
which elements are gases at room temperature | list of gas elements | how many elements are gases => Eleven elements are gases at room temperature: hydrogen, helium, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, neon, chlorine, argon, krypton, xenon and radon.
what is the most common metal in earths crust | most abundant metal => Aluminium is the most common metal in Earth's crust, about 8% by mass. Iron is second.
what is the rarest element | rarest natural element | rarest element on earth => Astatine is the rarest naturally occurring element. Less than about a gram exists in Earth's crust at any moment, because it decays so fast.
what is the most expensive element | most valuable element | priciest metal => Among elements you can actually buy, rhodium is often the most expensive, costing more than gold. Radioactive elements made in labs cost far more per gram.
what elements are in the human body | what is the body made of elements | most common element in the body => By mass, your body is about 65% oxygen, 18% carbon, 10% hydrogen and 3% nitrogen, plus calcium, phosphorus and small amounts of many others.
what are the newest elements | when were the last elements named | elements 113 115 117 118 => The newest elements, 113, 115, 117 and 118, were officially named in 2016: nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson. That completed the seventh row.
will there be more elements | element 119 | can new elements be discovered => Scientists are trying to make element 119 and 120, which would start an eighth row. As of 2025, none has been confirmed.
what are synthetic elements | man made elements | elements made in labs => Synthetic elements are made by people in labs or reactors. Technetium (43) was the first, and almost all elements beyond uranium (92) are made this way.
why do elements have weird symbols | why is gold au | why is iron fe => Some symbols come from Latin names: Au from aurum (gold), Fe from ferrum (iron), Ag from argentum (silver), Pb from plumbum (lead) and Na from natrium (sodium).

what are the s p d and f blocks | blocks of the periodic table | what is the d block => The table is split into blocks by which electron orbital is being filled. The s block is groups 1 and 2, the p block is groups 13 to 18, the d block is the transition metals, and the f block is the lanthanides and actinides.
what is electronegativity | most electronegative element | electronegativity trend => Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. Fluorine is the most electronegative element. It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
what are periodic trends | atomic radius trend | how do atoms change across the periodic table => Going across a period, atoms get smaller and hold electrons more tightly. Going down a group, atoms get bigger and metals get more reactive.
why did mendeleev leave gaps | how did mendeleev predict elements | eka silicon => Mendeleev left gaps for elements nobody had found yet and predicted their properties. When gallium, scandium and germanium were found, they matched his predictions closely.

what are the lab safety rules | science lab rules | how to stay safe in a lab => Wear goggles, tie back long hair, never eat or drink in the lab, follow your teacher's instructions, report spills and breakages, and wash your hands afterward.
why do you wear goggles in a lab | when should you wear safety goggles | lab goggles => Goggles protect your eyes from splashes, flying glass and fumes. Eyes are easily damaged, so wear them whenever you heat things or use chemicals.
what do hazard symbols mean | chemical warning symbols | ghs pictograms => Hazard symbols warn of dangers: a flame means flammable, a skull means toxic, a corroded hand means corrosive, an exclamation mark means harmful or irritant, and an exploding bomb means explosive.
chemical in eye first aid | eye wash station => Rinse your eye with lots of clean water for at least 15 minutes, using an eyewash if there is one, and tell an adult right away.
what should you do if you spill acid on your skin | acid on skin => Rinse the area with lots of cool running water for at least 15 minutes, take off any splashed clothing, and tell your teacher or an adult straight away.
how do you smell a chemical safely | wafting in chemistry | why not sniff chemicals => Never sniff a chemical directly. Waft by gently waving your hand over the container toward your nose, so you only catch a little bit of the smell.
how do you light a bunsen burner | bunsen burner steps | bunsen burner safety => Close the air hole, light a match or splint, then turn on the gas and light it. Open the air hole for a hotter blue flame. Use the yellow safety flame when it's not in use.
what is the safety flame on a bunsen burner | blue flame vs yellow flame bunsen | bunsen burner flames => The yellow flame (air hole closed) is easy to see and cooler, so it's the safety flame. The blue roaring flame (air hole open) is much hotter and used for heating.
how do you heat a test tube safely | test tube heating | point test tube away => Hold the test tube with a holder at an angle, point the open end away from everyone, and heat it gently, moving it in the flame so it doesn't spit.
why should you add acid to water | do you add water to acid or acid to water | do what you oughta add acid to water => Always add acid slowly to water, not water to acid. Diluting strong acid gives off heat, and adding water to acid can make it boil and splash.
what are common lab equipment names | science lab equipment list | lab glassware names => Common lab equipment includes beakers, test tubes, conical (Erlenmeyer) flasks, measuring cylinders, pipettes, funnels, Bunsen burners, tripods, gauze mats and evaporating dishes.
what is a beaker | beaker vs flask | what is a conical flask => A beaker is a straight sided glass container for mixing and heating. A conical (Erlenmeyer) flask has a narrow neck, so it's easier to swirl without spilling.
what is a meniscus | how to read a measuring cylinder | read from the bottom of the meniscus => The meniscus is the curved surface of a liquid in a tube. For water, read the volume at the bottom of the curve, with your eye level with it.
what is a fume cupboard | what is a fume hood | why use a fume hood => A fume cupboard (fume hood) is an enclosed space with a fan that sucks away harmful gases, so you can work with smelly or toxic chemicals safely.
why shouldnt you mix bleach and ammonia | dangerous cleaning product mixes | bleach and vinegar => Mixing bleach with ammonia makes toxic chloramine gases, and mixing bleach with acids like vinegar releases toxic chlorine gas. Never mix cleaning products.
what is an msds | what is a safety data sheet | where to find chemical safety info => A safety data sheet (SDS) lists a chemical's hazards, how to store it safely, and first aid steps. Labs keep one for every chemical they use.
what is a chemist | what do chemists do | jobs in chemistry => A chemist studies what substances are made of and how they react. Chemists make medicines, materials, foods, fuels and test things like water safety.
what is alchemy | did alchemists turn lead into gold | alchemy vs chemistry => Alchemy was an early mix of science, philosophy and magic that tried things like turning lead into gold. It never worked, but alchemists invented useful lab tools and methods that led to chemistry.
can you turn lead into gold | is it possible to make gold | making gold in a lab => Yes, but only in a nuclear way. Particle accelerators have turned tiny amounts of lead or bismuth into gold atoms, but it costs far more than the gold is worth.
why is chemistry important | what is chemistry used for | why study chemistry => Chemistry explains what everything is made of and how it changes. It's behind medicine, cooking, cleaning, fuels, plastics, batteries, farming and keeping water safe.
what is a mole ratio | how do you use moles in equations | mole calculations => In a balanced equation, the big numbers give the ratio of moles. In 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, two moles of hydrogen react with one mole of oxygen to make two moles of water.
how do you calculate moles | moles formula | moles mass molar mass => Moles = mass in grams ÷ molar mass. For example, 36 grams of water ÷ 18 g/mol = 2 moles of water.
what is relative formula mass | how do you work out formula mass | mr in chemistry => Relative formula mass (Mr) is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in a formula. For CO2 it's 12 + 16 + 16 = 44.
what is percentage composition | how to find percent by mass of an element | percent of oxygen in water => Percent by mass = (mass of the element in the formula ÷ formula mass) x 100. Water is about 89% oxygen by mass (16 ÷ 18).
what is yield in chemistry | percent yield | why is yield less than 100 => Yield is how much product you actually get. Percent yield = actual ÷ expected x 100. It's usually below 100% because of spills, side reactions or reactions not finishing.
