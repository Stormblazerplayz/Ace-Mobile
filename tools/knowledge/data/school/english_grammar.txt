# English grammar: parts of speech, sentence parts and types, clauses, tenses, agreement. Written by hand.
# tag: school.english.parts-of-speech
what are the 8 parts of speech | list the parts of speech | parts of speech in english => The eight traditional parts of speech are nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections. Many teachers also count articles and other determiners.
what is a common noun | common noun examples | common noun definition => A common noun names a general person, place or thing, like city, dog or teacher. It isn't capitalized unless it starts a sentence.
what is a proper noun | proper noun examples | proper noun definition => A proper noun names a specific person, place or thing, like Maria, Paris or the Mississippi River. Proper nouns are always capitalized.
what is an abstract noun | abstract noun examples | abstract vs concrete noun => An abstract noun names an idea, feeling or quality you can't touch, like courage, freedom or happiness. A concrete noun names something you can sense, like rock, music or pizza.
what is a collective noun | collective noun examples | group noun => A collective noun names a group as one unit, like team, family, class, flock or herd.
what is a compound noun | compound noun examples | two words make one noun => A compound noun is made of two or more words, like toothbrush, ice cream or mother-in-law. Some are closed, some open and some hyphenated.
what is a possessive noun | how do you make a noun possessive | apostrophe s for ownership => A possessive noun shows ownership. Add 's to most singular nouns (the dog's bowl) and just an apostrophe to plurals ending in s (the dogs' bowls).
what is a countable noun | uncountable noun | count vs noncount nouns => Countable nouns can be counted and have plurals, like apple and apples. Uncountable nouns can't, like water, rice or advice; you say "some water", not "two waters" (except when meaning servings).
how do you make words plural | plural rules | plural of words ending in y => Usually add -s. Add -es after s, x, z, ch or sh (boxes, wishes). For consonant + y, change y to i and add -es (baby → babies); for vowel + y just add -s (toys).
what are irregular plurals | irregular plural nouns | plural of child => Some nouns change in unusual ways: child → children, man → men, woman → women, foot → feet, tooth → teeth, mouse → mice, person → people, goose → geese.
what is the plural of words ending in f | plural of leaf | plural of knife => Many words ending in f or fe change to -ves: leaf → leaves, knife → knives, wolf → wolves, life → lives. Some just add -s, like roof → roofs and chef → chefs.
what is the plural of words ending in o | plural of potato | plural of piano => Some add -es (potatoes, tomatoes, heroes, echoes) and others add just -s (pianos, photos, radios, zoos). It's worth memorizing the -es ones.
what nouns are the same in singular and plural | plural of sheep | plural of fish => Some nouns don't change: one sheep, two sheep; one deer, two deer; one moose, two moose. "Fish" is normally the same, though "fishes" is used for different kinds.
what is a personal pronoun | personal pronouns list | subject pronouns => Personal pronouns stand for people or things. Subject pronouns are I, you, he, she, it, we and they; object pronouns are me, you, him, her, it, us and them.
what is a possessive pronoun | possessive pronouns list | mine yours his hers => Possessive pronouns show ownership and stand alone: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours and theirs. "The book is mine." They never use apostrophes.
what is a reflexive pronoun | reflexive pronouns list | myself yourself => Reflexive pronouns end in -self or -selves and refer back to the subject: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves. "She taught herself to code."
what is a relative pronoun | relative pronouns list | who which that pronouns => Relative pronouns start a clause that describes a noun: who, whom, whose, which and that. "The girl who won is my friend."
what is a demonstrative pronoun | this that these those | demonstrative pronouns => Demonstrative pronouns point to things: this and these (near), that and those (far). "This is mine; those are yours."
what is an indefinite pronoun | indefinite pronouns list | everyone someone anybody => Indefinite pronouns refer to people or things in general: everyone, someone, anybody, nothing, each, few, many, all, some. Words like everyone and each take a singular verb.
what is an interrogative pronoun | question pronouns | who what which pronoun => Interrogative pronouns start questions: who, whom, whose, what and which. "Which do you want?"
when do you use i or me | is it me and my friend or my friend and i | me vs i => Use I as a subject and me as an object. Take out the other person to test: "Sam and I went" (I went) but "She called Sam and me" (she called me).
when do you use who or whom | who vs whom | whom meaning => Use who for the subject and whom for the object. Test with he or him: if "him" fits, use whom. "Who called?" (he called) and "To whom did you write?" (to him).
what is an antecedent | pronoun antecedent | what does antecedent mean in grammar => An antecedent is the noun a pronoun refers back to. In "Maya lost her pen," Maya is the antecedent of her.
what is pronoun antecedent agreement | pronoun agreement | everyone and their => A pronoun should match its antecedent in number. "The students packed their bags." With words like everyone, "their" is now widely accepted as singular, though some teachers prefer "his or her".
what is an action verb | action verb examples | action verbs list => An action verb shows what someone or something does, like run, think, write or jump.
what is a linking verb | linking verb examples | is is a linking verb => A linking verb connects the subject to a word that describes or renames it. Forms of "be" (am, is, are, was, were) are the most common, plus seem, become, feel, look and taste when they describe.
what is a helping verb | auxiliary verb | helping verbs list => A helping (auxiliary) verb comes before the main verb: am, is, are, was, were, be, been, have, has, had, do, does, did, can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must. "She has finished."
what is a modal verb | modal verbs list | can could may might => Modal verbs show ability, permission, possibility or need: can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would. They're followed by the base verb: "You should go."
what is a transitive verb | intransitive verb | transitive vs intransitive => A transitive verb takes a direct object (She kicked the ball). An intransitive verb doesn't (She laughed). Some verbs can be both.
what is an irregular verb | irregular verbs list | regular vs irregular verbs => Regular verbs add -ed for the past (walk → walked). Irregular verbs change differently: go → went → gone, eat → ate → eaten, see → saw → seen, take → took → taken.
what are the principal parts of a verb | verb forms | base past past participle => The principal parts are the base (sing), past (sang), past participle (sung) and present participle (singing).
what is a past participle | past participle examples | have eaten or have ate => A past participle is the verb form used after has, have or had, or in the passive: "I have eaten" (not "have ate"), "it was written".
what is a gerund | gerund examples | verb ending in ing used as a noun => A gerund is an -ing verb form used as a noun. In "Swimming is fun," swimming is a gerund and the subject.
what is an infinitive | infinitive examples | to plus verb => An infinitive is "to" plus the base verb, like to run or to learn. It can act as a noun, adjective or adverb: "I want to learn."
what is a participle | participle adjective | present participle => A participle is a verb form used as an adjective. The present participle ends in -ing (a running dog); the past participle often ends in -ed or -en (a broken vase).
what is a split infinitive | is it wrong to split an infinitive | to boldly go => A split infinitive puts a word between "to" and the verb, as in "to boldly go". It's fine in modern English, though some style guides avoid it in formal writing.
what is a comparative adjective | superlative adjective | comparative and superlative => Comparatives compare two things (taller, more careful); superlatives compare three or more (tallest, most careful). Use -er/-est for short words and more/most for most longer ones.
what are irregular comparatives | good better best | bad worse worst => A few adjectives change completely: good → better → best, bad → worse → worst, many/much → more → most, little → less → least, far → farther/further → farthest/furthest.
what is the order of adjectives | adjective order in english | why is it big red ball => English adjectives usually go in this order: opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose. So "a lovely little old round red French wooden mixing bowl".
what are articles in grammar | a an the | definite and indefinite articles => The articles are a, an and the. "The" is definite (a specific thing); "a" and "an" are indefinite (any one thing).
when do you use a or an | a vs an | an before vowel sounds => Use "an" before a vowel sound and "a" before a consonant sound. It's the sound that matters: an hour, an MP3, a university, a one-time deal.
what is a determiner | determiners examples | what are determiners in grammar => A determiner comes before a noun to tell which one or how many: articles (a, the), demonstratives (this, those), possessives (my, their), and quantifiers (some, many, every).
what is an adverb of frequency | always usually sometimes never | frequency adverbs => Adverbs of frequency tell how often: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never. They usually go before the main verb: "I always walk."
do all adverbs end in ly | adverbs without ly | is fast an adverb => No. Many adverbs end in -ly (quickly), but others don't: fast, well, very, soon, often, never, here, there and too. And some -ly words, like friendly, are adjectives.
when do you use good or well | good vs well | i'm good or i'm well => Good is an adjective and well is usually an adverb: "a good singer" but "she sings well". After linking verbs, "I'm good" is fine, and "I'm well" is used about health.
what is a preposition | prepositions list | preposition examples => A preposition shows how a noun relates to other words, often in place or time: in, on, at, under, over, between, before, after, with, from, to, during.
can you end a sentence with a preposition | ending sentences with prepositions | preposition at the end of a sentence => Yes. "Who are you talking to?" is normal English. The old rule against it came from Latin and isn't followed by modern grammar guides.
what is a prepositional phrase | prepositional phrase examples | preposition and its object => A prepositional phrase is a preposition plus its object and any modifiers, like "under the old bridge" or "after lunch".
what are coordinating conjunctions | fanboys | for and nor but or yet so => The coordinating conjunctions are FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. They join words or independent clauses of equal weight.
what are subordinating conjunctions | subordinating conjunction list | because although when => Subordinating conjunctions start a dependent clause: because, although, since, unless, when, while, if, after, before, until. "I stayed inside because it rained."
what are correlative conjunctions | either or neither nor | both and not only but also => Correlative conjunctions come in pairs: either/or, neither/nor, both/and, not only/but also, whether/or. "Neither Ann nor Ben came."
can you start a sentence with and | starting a sentence with but | is it wrong to start with because => Yes, you can start a sentence with and, but or because. It's grammatical, but use it on purpose and not too often, and make sure a "because" sentence is complete.
what is an interjection | interjection examples | wow ouch oops => An interjection shows sudden feeling and stands apart from the sentence: wow, ouch, oops, hey, yikes. It's often followed by an exclamation mark or comma.
# tag: school.english.sentences
what is a subject in a sentence | how do you find the subject | subject of a sentence => The subject is who or what the sentence is about, usually doing the action. Find the verb, then ask "who or what?" In "The tired dog slept," the subject is "the tired dog".
what is a predicate | predicate of a sentence | complete predicate vs simple predicate => The predicate tells what the subject does or is. The complete predicate is the verb and everything with it; the simple predicate is just the verb. In "Mia ran to school," "ran to school" is the complete predicate.
what is a direct object | direct object examples | how to find the direct object => A direct object receives the action of the verb. Ask "what?" or "whom?" after the verb: in "Leo threw the ball," the ball is the direct object.
what is an indirect object | indirect object examples | how to find the indirect object => An indirect object tells to whom or for whom something is done. In "Leo threw Max the ball," Max is the indirect object and the ball is the direct object.
what is a subject complement | predicate nominative | predicate adjective => A subject complement follows a linking verb and describes or renames the subject. In "She is a pilot," pilot renames her; in "She is brave," brave describes her.
what is a compound subject | compound predicate | two subjects one verb => A compound subject has two or more subjects sharing a verb (Tom and Ana sang). A compound predicate has two or more verbs sharing a subject (Tom sang and danced).
what is a clause | define clause in grammar | clause vs phrase => A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. A phrase is a group of words without both, like "in the morning".
what is an independent clause | main clause | independent clause examples => An independent clause has a subject and verb and makes sense on its own as a sentence. "The bell rang" is an independent clause.
what is a dependent clause | subordinate clause | dependent clause examples => A dependent clause has a subject and verb but can't stand alone, because it starts with a word like because, when or although. "When the bell rang" needs more to be complete.
what is a relative clause | adjective clause | relative clause examples => A relative clause describes a noun and starts with who, which, that, whose or whom. "The book that I borrowed was great."
that vs which | restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses => In American English, "that" starts essential information with no commas (the car that I bought), and "which" starts extra information with commas (my car, which is red, is fast).
what is an appositive | appositive examples | appositive phrase => An appositive is a noun or phrase next to another noun that renames it, often set off by commas. "My dog, a lazy beagle, sleeps all day."
what is a simple sentence | simple sentence examples | one independent clause => A simple sentence has one independent clause, like "The cat slept." It can still have compound subjects or verbs.
what is a compound sentence | compound sentence examples | two independent clauses => A compound sentence joins two independent clauses with a comma and a FANBOYS word, or with a semicolon. "I studied, so I passed."
what is a complex sentence | complex sentence examples | independent and dependent clause => A complex sentence has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. "Because I studied, I passed."
what is a compound complex sentence | compound complex sentence examples | two independent and one dependent => A compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause. "Because it rained, the game stopped, and we went home."
what are the four types of sentences | declarative interrogative imperative exclamatory | kinds of sentences => Declarative sentences make statements (.), interrogative sentences ask questions (?), imperative sentences give commands (. or !), and exclamatory sentences show strong feeling (!).
what is an imperative sentence | command sentence | imperative sentence examples => An imperative sentence gives a command or request, like "Close the door." The subject "you" is understood but not written.
what is a sentence fragment | how do you fix a fragment | incomplete sentence => A fragment is missing a subject, a verb or a complete thought, like "Because I was late." Fix it by joining it to a sentence or adding what's missing: "I ran because I was late."
what is a run on sentence | how do you fix a run on | fused sentence => A run-on joins two independent clauses with no punctuation, like "I love pizza it's tasty." Fix it with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus FANBOYS word.
what is a comma splice | how to fix a comma splice | comma splice examples => A comma splice joins two independent clauses with only a comma: "It was late, we went home." Fix it with a period, a semicolon, or ", so".
what is subject verb agreement | subject verb agreement rules | singular subject singular verb => The verb must match the subject in number: "The dog barks" but "The dogs bark". Ignore phrases in between: "The box of apples is heavy."
is it neither of them is or are | each of the students is or are | everyone is or are => Words like each, every, either, neither, everyone and nobody are singular: "Each of the students is ready." "Neither of them is here" is standard, though "are" is common in speech.
is it the team is or the team are | collective noun verb agreement | family is or family are => In American English, collective nouns usually take a singular verb: "The team is winning." British English often uses plural: "The team are winning."
what is a double negative | why are double negatives wrong | i don't have nothing => A double negative uses two negatives in one clause, like "I don't have nothing." In standard English, use one: "I don't have anything" or "I have nothing."
what is a misplaced modifier | misplaced modifier examples | dangling modifier => A modifier should sit next to what it describes. "Walking home, the rain soaked me" is a dangling modifier (the rain wasn't walking). Fix it: "Walking home, I got soaked by the rain."
what is parallel structure | parallelism in grammar | parallel structure examples => Parallel structure means using the same grammar pattern in a list or pair. Say "I like hiking, swimming and biking," not "hiking, swimming and to bike."
what is active voice | passive voice | active vs passive voice => In active voice the subject does the action (The dog chased the cat). In passive voice the subject receives it (The cat was chased by the dog). Active is usually clearer and stronger.
how do you change passive to active voice | fix passive voice | rewrite in active voice => Find who does the action and make it the subject. "The cake was eaten by Sam" becomes "Sam ate the cake."
what is the subjunctive mood | if i were or if i was | subjunctive examples => The subjunctive is used for wishes and unreal situations: "If I were you..." and "I wish it were Friday." It's also used after demands: "I suggest that he leave."
# tag: school.english.tenses
what are the verb tenses in english | how many tenses are there in english | list of tenses => English has three main times (past, present, future), each with four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous). That makes 12 common tense forms.
what is the simple present tense | present simple | when to use simple present => The simple present shows habits, facts and general truths: "She walks to school." "Water boils at 100°C." Add -s for he, she and it.
what is the present continuous tense | present progressive | am is are plus ing => The present continuous shows something happening now: am/is/are + -ing. "I am reading."
what is the present perfect tense | have plus past participle | present perfect examples => The present perfect links the past to now: has/have + past participle. "I have finished my homework." "She has lived here for years."
what is the simple past tense | past simple | when to use simple past => The simple past shows a finished action at a past time: "We played yesterday." Regular verbs add -ed.
what is the past continuous tense | past progressive => The past continuous shows an action in progress in the past: was/were + -ing. "I was sleeping when you called."
what is the past perfect tense | past perfect examples => The past perfect shows an action finished before another past action: had + past participle. "The movie had started when we arrived."
what is the future tense | will vs going to | how to talk about the future => English uses will (I will help), going to (I'm going to help), and the present continuous for plans (I'm leaving tomorrow).
what is the future perfect tense | future perfect examples => The future perfect shows something done before a future time: will have + past participle. "By June, I will have finished the book."
what is the present perfect continuous | present perfect progressive => It shows an action that started in the past and is still going: has/have been + -ing. "I have been waiting for an hour."
what is tense consistency | shifting tenses in writing | keep the same tense => Keep the same tense unless the time really changes. Don't switch between "she walks" and "she walked" in the same story without a reason.
what is the past tense of dive | dived or dove | past tense of sneak => Both dived and dove are correct, with dove more common in American English. Likewise sneaked and snuck are both used, though sneaked is more formal.
